Introduction: Two Paths to One Goal

At the very heart of every still lies the same physical principle: heat a liquid until the alcohol vaporizes, carry the vapor away, and condense it in a cooled state. But how that principle is implemented determines whether you get an aromatic calvados with the character of a Norman orchard or a crystal-clear neutral spirit for a tincture.

Distillation and rectification are not two different processes. They are two depths of the same process of separating liquids by boiling point. Distillation takes one step. Rectification takes dozens or hundreds of steps within a single apparatus. The difference between them is like sifting flour once through a sieve versus passing it through a cascade of forty sieves with progressively smaller mesh.

⚗️ Distillation

One evaporation, one condensation. Preserves the aroma of the raw material. The method of cognac, calvados, grappa, slivovitz, and whiskey.

1 theoretical plate · 25–70% ABV

🏭 Rectification

Dozens of repeated evaporation-condensation cycles in a column. Produces pure neutral spirit. The method of vodka, tinctures, gin, and liqueurs.

20–60 theoretical plates · 92–96% ABV

For the distiller, the choice between these methods determines everything: the type of apparatus, the character of the product, the working style, the number of runs, and ultimately what ends up in the glass. This article is a detailed breakdown of both methods — from the physics of evaporation to practical equipment recommendations.

Chapter 1. The Physics: Why Alcohol Separates from Water

1.1. The Difference in Boiling Points

At the core of any distillation lies the difference in boiling points of the components in the mixture:

Substance Boiling Point (at 760 mmHg)
Methanol 64.7 °C
Acetone 56.1 °C
Ethyl acetate 77.1 °C
Ethanol 78.37 °C
Water 100.0 °C
Isoamyl alcohol 132.0 °C
Furfural 161.7 °C

When the wash is heated, the component with the lowest boiling point evaporates first. Ethanol boils at 78.37 °C — significantly lower than water (100 °C). So when the wash is heated to 78–85 °C, the vapor becomes enriched with ethanol relative to the liquid in the boiler. By condensing this vapor, we obtain a liquid with a higher alcohol concentration than the original wash.

1.2. The Problem: The Azeotrope

If separation were perfect, we could obtain 100% ethanol. But this is impossible because of the azeotrope.

⚠️ The Ethanol-Water Azeotrope

An azeotrope is a mixture of two liquids that boils at a constant temperature and evaporates without a change in composition. For ethanol and water, the azeotrope is:

  • 95.6% ethanol and 4.4% water by mass (95.57% ethanol).
  • Boiling point: 78.15 °C — slightly below pure ethanol's boiling point (78.37 °C).

This means no distillation or rectification at atmospheric pressure can produce alcohol stronger than 96% ABV. Obtaining absolute alcohol (99.5%+) requires special methods: azeotropic distillation with benzene, molecular sieves, or membrane separation.

1.3. What Is a Theoretical Plate

To understand the difference between distillation and rectification, we need the concept of the theoretical plate (theoretical separation stage).

A theoretical plate is an idealized element where the vapor and liquid reach full thermodynamic equilibrium. On each plate, the vapor becomes enriched with the more volatile component (ethanol), while the liquid becomes enriched with the less volatile component (water).

⚗️ Simple Distillation

1
theoretical plate

Vapor rises from the boiler, contacts the condensate once, and goes to the condenser.

🏭 Rectification Column

20–60
theoretical plates

Vapor contacts the liquid repeatedly, becoming richer in ethanol each time.

The more theoretical plates, the purer the product at the output.

Chapter 2. Simple Distillation: One Step to Concentrate

⚗️

Simple Distillation One evaporation, one condensation — the traditional method

2.1. Operating Principle

Simple distillation is a single evaporation and condensation. The wash is heated in the boiler, the vapor rises, passes through the vapor duct (and possibly a short dephlegmator), enters the condenser, and condenses into the receiving vessel.

There is no element of repeated vapor-liquid contact between the boiler and the condenser. The vapor formed above the surface of the wash immediately goes to condensation. The vapor composition is determined by the equilibrium between the liquid in the boiler and the vapor above it — one equilibrium, one theoretical plate.

2.2. What You Get at the Output

From a wash of 10–12% ABV, simple distillation yields a distillate of 25–40% ABV (depending on when collection stops). This is low wines — a concentrate containing:

  • Ethanol.
  • Water.
  • Head fractions (methanol, acetaldehyde, ethyl acetate, acetone).
  • Tail fractions (fusel oils: isoamyl, isobutyl, propyl alcohols; furfural).
  • Aromatic compounds of the raw material (esters, terpenes, aldehydes).
Low wines cannot be drunk without a second distillation. They contain toxic head fractions and coarse tail impurities. But they are excellent raw material for fractional distillation.

2.3. Fractional Distillation: Two Steps Instead of One

Fractional distillation is simple distillation in which the distillate is separated into fractions as it is collected:

Heads
5–10% of abs. alcohol
Methanol, acetaldehyde, ethyl acetate. Sharp, unpleasant smell. Not consumable.
Hearts
Main portion
Ethanol with a moderate amount of aromatic impurities. The target product.
Tails
Last 10–15%
Fusel oils, furfural, fatty acids. Oily, unpleasant smell. Not consumable.

Fractional distillation is still simple distillation (one theoretical plate), but with manual separation of the distillate into fractions as its composition changes. The separation happens not inside the apparatus, but in time: the distiller switches receiving vessels, guided by boiler temperature, stream strength, and smell.

2.4. Equipment for Simple and Fractional Distillation

Minimal apparatus:

  • Still boiler (10–50 liters) with lid and thermometer.
  • Vapor duct or coil.
  • Condenser (Liebig, Dimroth, or shell-and-tube).
  • Receiving vessels.

Optional elements:

  • Thump keg (thumper): an intermediate vessel between the boiler and condenser. Retains splashes and part of the tail fractions. Increases distillate strength by 2–5%.
  • Dephlegmator (short column 10–20 cm): creates partial reflux, increasing strength and purity. Equivalent to 2–3 theoretical plates.
  • Copper elements: copper boiler, copper mesh in the vapor duct. Copper binds sulfur compounds, softening the profile.

Cost of a basic apparatus: €100–300.

✅ 2.5. Advantages
  • Preserves aroma. One theoretical plate does not separate the raw material's aromatic compounds. Terpenes, esters, and aldehydes carry over into the distillate, forming the characteristic bouquet. Critical for calvados, grappa, slivovitz, whiskey.
  • Simple equipment. No column, packing, or take-off node needed. Fewer elements, fewer points of failure.
  • Speed. One run takes 2–4 hours. Two runs (simple + fractional) — 4–8 hours. Rectification of one batch — 8–16 hours.
  • Flexibility. The same apparatus works with any raw material: sugar, grain, fruit, berries, honey.
  • Tradition. All classic European distillates (cognac, calvados, grappa, slivovitz, pálinka, whiskey) are produced by simple distillation.
❌ 2.6. Disadvantages
  • Limited strength. Maximum 65–70% ABV in the hearts on the second fractional run. Impossible to obtain 90+% ABV.
  • Incomplete separation. Head and tail fractions partially overlap with the hearts. Complete removal of impurities is impossible.
  • Requires two runs. First (simple) to concentrate, second (fractional) to purify. Increases time and losses.
  • Skill-dependent. The quality of fraction separation is determined by the distiller's experience: when to switch vessels, how many heads to take, where the boundary between hearts and tails lies.

Chapter 3. Rectification: Repeated Separation in a Column

🏭

Rectification Multi-stage separation for maximum purity

3.1. Operating Principle

Rectification is repeated evaporation and condensation inside a column. Vapor from the boiler rises up the column, contacting the descending liquid (reflux) on each theoretical plate. On each plate:

  1. The vapor gives up heavy components (water, fusel oils) to the liquid.
  2. The liquid gives up light components (ethanol, methanol) to the vapor.
  3. The vapor, rising to the next plate, becomes richer in ethanol.
  4. The liquid, flowing down, becomes richer in water and heavy impurities.

The key element is the reflux: part of the condensed vapor returns to the column and flows down, counter to the rising vapor. This counterflow creates repeated contact and ensures separation.

3.2. The Reflux Ratio

The reflux ratio (R) is the ratio of the amount of reflux returned to the column to the amount of distillate collected in the receiving vessel.

R = 1
Fast
Half the condensate returns, half is collected. Fast collection, low purity.
R = 3
Moderate
75% returns, 25% collected. Moderate speed, good purity.
R = 5
Slow
83% returns, 17% collected. Slow collection, high purity.
R = ∞
Total Reflux
All condensate returns, no collection. Used to stabilize the column before collection.
The higher the reflux ratio, the purer the product, but the slower the process. To obtain 96% alcohol requires R = 3–5 and 30–50 theoretical plates.

3.3. Column Types and Packing

Column packing is the material filling the column pipe and creating the surface for vapor-liquid contact. Each unit of packing of a certain height is equivalent to one theoretical plate.

Packing Type HETP* (cm) Features
Spiral (springs) 3–5 cm High efficiency, low resistance
Raschig rings 5–8 cm Classic, simple to manufacture
Berl saddles 4–6 cm Good contact, moderate resistance
Panchenkov packing (RPN) 4–7 cm Accessible, popular in CIS
Copper mesh 5–8 cm Binds sulfur compounds
Plate column 5–10 cm/plate Industrial standard, complex to build

*HETP — Height Equivalent to a Theoretical Plate. The lower the HETP, the more efficient the packing.

To obtain 96% alcohol:

  • Packing with HETP of 4 cm.
  • Column height: 120–200 cm.
  • Theoretical plates: 30–50.

3.4. What You Get at the Output

From a wash of 10–12% ABV, rectification yields a rectificate of 92–96% ABV. This is practically pure ethanol with impurity content:

  • Methanol: < 0.01% (with proper heads collection).
  • Fusel oils: < 0.005%.
  • Aldehydes: < 0.003%.
  • Esters: traces.

The rectificate is a neutral spirit with no aroma or taste of the raw material. The ideal base for vodka, tinctures, liqueurs, and gin.

3.5. Equipment for Rectification

Minimal rectification apparatus:

  • Still boiler (20–50 liters) with thermometer and pressure gauge.
  • Rectification column (pipe of 28–50 mm diameter, 120–200 cm height).
  • Packing (spiral, RPN, Raschig rings).
  • Dephlegmator (condenser at the top of the column).
  • Take-off node (valve or needle valve for controlling collection).
  • Condenser (Liebig or shell-and-tube).
  • Thermometers: in the boiler and at the top of the column.
  • Receiving vessels.

Cost of a rectification apparatus: €300–1,000 (depending on quality, column diameter, and configuration).

✅ 3.6. Advantages
  • Maximum purity. 96% ethanol with minimal impurities. Unattainable with simple distillation.
  • One run instead of two. Rectification from the wash directly yields clean spirit. No need for low wines and a second run.
  • Complete removal of toxic impurities. Methanol, acetaldehyde, and fusel oils are effectively separated and collected as heads and tails.
  • Reproducibility. The process is determined by physical parameters (reflux ratio, temperature, pressure), not the distiller's subjective intuition.
  • Time savings in the long run. One rectification (8–12 hours) replaces two simple distillations (6–8 hours + cleaning).
❌ 3.7. Disadvantages
  • Loss of aroma. 30–50 theoretical plates separate not only impurities but also the raw material's aromatic compounds. A rectificate from grape wash does not smell of grapes. From apple — not of apples. The aroma is destroyed.
  • Complex equipment. Column, packing, dephlegmator, take-off node — more elements, more points of failure, more parameters to control.
  • Long process. Column stabilization (30–60 min) + collection (6–10 hours) = 8–12 hours per batch.
  • High cost. A quality rectification column costs 3–5 times more than a simple still boiler.
  • Demanding on the wash. A cloudy wash with suspended matter clogs the packing. Thorough clarification or filtration is required before rectification.
  • Limited applicability. For aromatic distillates (whiskey, calvados, grappa, slivovitz), rectification is unsuitable. It destroys the very reason for using aromatic raw material.

Chapter 5. Which Method to Choose: Practical Recommendations

🍬 Sugar Wash (Neutral Spirit)

Goal: clean ethanol with no smell or taste for tinctures, liqueurs, vodka.

🏭 Rectification. Sugar wash has no aromatic value. The goal is maximum clean spirit. Rectification yields 96% ethanol in one run. Alternative: two fractional runs on a simple apparatus (longer, but cheaper equipment).

🌾 Grain Wash (Whiskey, Polugar, Bourbon)

Goal: preserve the bready, malty, grainy character.

⚗️ Two fractional runs on a simple apparatus. Grain aroma is the main value. Rectification would destroy it. Classic scheme: first run to 25–30% without cuts, second fractional with heads and tails, hearts to 60–65% in the stream.

🍎 Fruit Wash (Calvados, Grappa, Chacha, Slivovitz)

Goal: preserve and enhance the fruit aroma.

⚗️ Two fractional runs on a simple apparatus. Fruit aromatic compounds (terpenes, esters, lactones) are extremely volatile and delicate. Rectification would separate them and destroy the bouquet. Only simple distillation with gentle heads collection (3–5%) and hearts to 60–65% ABV.

🍒 Berry Wash (Kirschwasser, Raspberry Brandy)

Goal: preserve the fragile berry aroma.

⚗️ Two fractional runs with copper elements. Berry aromas (raspberry ketone, ionones, benzaldehyde) are the most delicate. One extra theoretical plate can lose them. Only a simple boiler, minimal dephlegmation, hearts at 55–60%.

🍯 Honey Wash

Goal: preserve the floral, waxy, spicy honey profile.

⚗️ Two fractional runs on a simple apparatus. Similar to fruit wash. Honey aromas (phenylethanol, complex esters, waxy compounds) are lost during rectification.

🧪 Neutral Spirit for Infusions

Goal: clean base for tinctures, gin, absinthe, liqueurs.

🏭 Rectification. If you plan to infuse spirit on herbs, berries, spices, or pass it through a gin basket, you need the cleanest possible base. A 96% rectificate is the ideal choice.

Chapter 6. Hybrid Solutions: Column with Reflux Control

6.1. The Concept

Modern stills are often a hybrid between a simple boiler and a rectification column. A short column (30–60 cm) with packing and an adjustable dephlegmator allows you to:

  • At minimal reflux (R = 0–1): work like a simple boiler — aroma preserved, strength 55–65%.
  • At maximal reflux (R = 4–6): work like a rectification column — purity 90–95%, aroma lost.

6.2. Practical Application

For aromatic distillates:

  • Column 30–40 cm, copper or stainless packing.
  • Dephlegmator off or at minimum.
  • Reflux ratio: 0–1.
  • Hearts strength: 55–65%.
  • Result: aromatic distillate with moderate purity.

For neutral spirit:

  • Column 100–150 cm, spiral or RPN packing.
  • Dephlegmator at maximum.
  • Reflux ratio: 3–5.
  • Strength: 92–96%.
  • Result: clean rectificate.
✅ 6.3. Hybrid Advantages
  • One apparatus for all tasks. No need to buy two separate apparatuses.
  • Flexibility. Switching between modes takes 5–10 minutes.
  • Economy. A hybrid apparatus (€200–600) is cheaper than two separate ones (boiler + column: €400–1,300).
❌ 6.4. Hybrid Limitations
  • Not ideal in any mode. As a simple boiler, it is inferior to a classic alembic for aroma preservation. As a rectification column, it is inferior to a full-size column for purity.
  • Compromise. For serious whiskey or neutral spirit production, a specialized apparatus is better.

Chapter 7. Fractional Distillation: The Art of Separation

7.1. Why Fractional Distillation Is Not Just "Turn On and Collect"

Fractional distillation requires active participation from the distiller: they determine when to switch receiving vessels, guided by:

  • Boiler temperature. Heads are collected at 78–82 °C, hearts at 82–92 °C, tails at 92–99 °C.
  • Stream strength. Measured with an alcoholmeter in the receiving vessel. Heads: 80+%, hearts: 60–75%, tails: below 40%.
  • Smell. Heads smell of acetone, solvent. Hearts — of alcohol and the raw material. Tails — of fusel oil, oiliness.
  • Taste. Heads are burning, sharp. Hearts are soft. Tails are oily, bitter.

7.2. Fraction Volumes

For 20 liters of wash at 10% ABV (2 liters of absolute alcohol):

Fraction Volume % of Abs. Alcohol Contains
Heads 100–200 ml 5–10% Methanol, acetaldehyde, ethyl acetate
Hearts 1,400–1,600 ml (at 65%) 70–80% Ethanol + aromatic impurities
Tails 200–400 ml 10–20% Fusel oils, furfural
Boiler residue — — Water, non-volatile substances

7.3. Effect of Raw Material Type on Fractional Distillation

  • Sugar wash: heads and tails are clearly defined, the hearts boundary is obvious. Heads collection: 10%. Hearts to 65–70% in the stream.
  • Grain wash: aromatic impurities are distributed more widely. The hearts boundary is blurred. Heads collection: 5–8%. Hearts to 60–65% in the stream. Don't rush the tails cut — some aromatic compounds fall into the transition zone.
  • Fruit wash: aromatic compounds are in the early fractions. Heads collection: 3–5%. Hearts to 60–65%. An early heads cut is critical: too large a collection will lose the fruit esters.

Chapter 8. Typical Mistakes

Mistake Consequence Solution
Rectifying a fruit wash Loss of aroma, neutral spirit Use fractional distillation
Simple distillation without fraction cuts Methanol and fusel oil in the product Always do fractional distillation
Too large a heads collection for fruit wash Loss of aromatic esters For fruit: 3–5%, not 10%
Rectification without clarifying the wash Clogged packing, scorching Clarify the wash before rectification
Low reflux ratio during rectification Impure product, 80–85% instead of 96% Increase reflux ratio to 3–5
Collecting hearts above 70% during fractional distillation Fusel oils in the distillate Stop collection at 60–65%
Using rectificate for an herbal tincture expecting raw-material aroma Tincture on pure spirit without raw-material character This is normal: rectificate is the base; the raw material provides the aroma
Simple distillation without a thermometer Inability to control fractions Install a thermometer in the boiler and at the top
Rectification at atmospheric pressure in the mountains Azeotrope shifts, strength below 96% Account for altitude above sea level

With regular production (4–8 batches per month):

  • Two fractional runs: cost of 1 liter of distillate at 40–45% ABV — €1.5–3 (excluding raw materials).
  • Rectification: cost of 1 liter of rectificate at 96% ABV — €2–4 (excluding raw materials).
Rectification is more expensive in energy and water, but yields more pure spirit per liter of wash. For neutral spirit, it is economically more advantageous. For aromatic distillates, it is not applicable.

Chapter 10. Legal Context

In EU countries and beyond, legislation on home distillation varies:

  • Germany, Austria, France: home distillation requires registration and excise duty payment. Rectification columns above a certain power threshold may be prohibited for private use.
  • Italy, Spain, Portugal: limited volumes for personal consumption are permitted, subject to notification.
  • Scandinavian countries: home distillation is generally prohibited.
  • Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria: quotas and traditions exist, but volume and strength restrictions apply.
In some countries, rectification columns are regulated separately from simple still boilers, because they allow production of alcohol above 90% ABV, which may be equated to industrial production. Check the current regulations of your country before purchasing equipment.

Conclusion: The Method Is Determined by the Goal

The choice between distillation and rectification is not a question of "what is better." It is a question of "what do you want to get?"

If your goal is an aromatic distillate with the character of the raw material (whiskey, calvados, grappa, slivovitz, chacha, honey brandy), your method is simple fractional distillation. One or two theoretical plates, gentle heads collection, hearts at 55–65%. Aroma is the main value, and rectification will destroy it.

If your goal is a clean neutral spirit for vodka, tinctures, liqueurs, gin, your method is rectification. Thirty to fifty theoretical plates, reflux ratio 3–5, strength 92–96%. Purity is the main value, and simple distillation will not provide it.

If your goal is versatility, your method is a hybrid apparatus with adjustable reflux. For aromatic washes — simple boiler mode. For sugar wash — rectification mode. One apparatus, two styles.

Three Rules to Remember

🍎 Aromatic Raw Material → Simple Distillation Fruit, berries, grain, honey. Rectification will kill the bouquet.
🍬 Sugar and Neutrality → Rectification Clean spirit for tinctures and vodka. Simple distillation will not give 96%.
⚠️ Fractional Distillation Is Mandatory in Both Cases Never drink low wines without separating fractions. The heads are toxic. The tails are unpleasant. The hearts are what you are looking for.

Understanding the physics of the process turns the choice of method from guessing into a conscious decision. And a conscious decision is the difference between "I'm making moonshine" and "I'm creating a drink."

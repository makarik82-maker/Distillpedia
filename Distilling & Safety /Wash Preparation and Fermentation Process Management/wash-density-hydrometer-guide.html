Introduction: Numbers Instead of Guesses

"Is it fermenting or not?" "How much sugar is left?" "How much alcohol will I get?" — three questions every distiller asks while staring at the fermentation vessel. Without instruments, the answers are built on guesses: taste, the appearance of bubbles, intuition. With instruments — on numbers.

The saccharometer and the hydrometer are the two simplest and most important tools in a distiller's arsenal. A glass tube costing 5–15 euros, dipped into a graduated cylinder with wash, gives more information than a week of watching the airlock. Density is an objective indicator that allows you to:

  • Calculate how much sugar has been added and how much potential alcohol will be produced.
  • Track fermentation dynamics in real time.
  • Determine the moment of completion with an accuracy of ±0.5 °Plato.
  • Calculate the actual strength of the wash without distillation.
  • Assess the completeness of fermentation and take timely action if it stops.
  • Plan the volume and strength of the future distillate.

This article is a practical guide to working with density-measuring instruments. We will examine the physics of the process, the measurement scales, operating technique, calculation formulas, and typical mistakes. Everything you need to move from "eyeballing" to precise process control.

Chapter 1. The Physics of the Process: What the Hydrometer Measures

1.1. Archimedes' Principle and the Operating Principle

The hydrometer works on the basis of Archimedes' principle: a body immersed in a liquid displaces a volume of liquid equal to its weight. The denser the liquid, the more it pushes the body up, and the higher it floats.

Structurally, a hydrometer is a sealed glass tube with a weight at the bottom (usually shot or mercury) and a scale at the top. When immersed in a liquid, the hydrometer sinks to a certain depth, which depends on the density of the liquid. The density value is read from the liquid level on the scale.

Key point: the hydrometer does not measure sugar directly — it measures the overall density of the solution. All dissolved substances affect density: sugar, alcohol, salts, acids, proteins. But in a sugar wash, sugar makes the dominant contribution, so the hydrometer's readings reflect the sugar concentration with high accuracy.

1.2. What Happens to Density During Fermentation

Before fermentation, density is determined by sugar: the more sugar, the higher the density. During fermentation, yeast converts sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide:

🔄 Fermentation Reaction
Sugar (heavy) → Ethanol (light) + CO₂ (escapes)

The density of ethanol (0.789 g/ml) is lower than the density of water (1.000 g/ml), and the density of a sugar solution is higher. Therefore, as fermentation proceeds, density decreases: heavy sugar disappears, light alcohol appears, CO₂ escapes.

Stage Density (SG) Density (°P) What Is Happening
Start (after adding sugar) 1.075–1.090 18–22 Sugar dissolved
Active fermentation (day 2–3) 1.050–1.065 12–16 Sugar being consumed
Slowing (day 4–6) 1.020–1.040 5–10 Residual sugar
Completion (day 7–10) 0.995–1.010 0–2 Sugar fermented
Full finish (accounting for alcohol) 0.990–1.005 — Alcohol drops density below 1.000
Note: fully fermented wash has a density below 1.000 (below the density of water), because ethanol is lighter than water. This is an important diagnostic sign: if the wash density is below 1.000, sugar is practically completely fermented.

1.3. Why Density Is a Better Indicator Than Taste or Bubbles

  • Taste is subjective: different people perceive sweetness differently, and alcohol masks residual sugar.
  • Bubbles in the airlock depend on the seal of the vessel, temperature, and dissolved CO₂ — they do not reflect the real fermentation speed.
  • Density is objective, reproducible, and quantitative. Two measurements 24 hours apart give an exact answer: fermenting or not.

Chapter 2. Instruments: Hydrometer, Saccharometer, Refractometer

🌡️

Hydrometer (Specific Gravity)

The basic instrument for measuring liquid density. Uses an SG or °Plato scale.

  • Range: 0.990–1.120
  • Accuracy: ±0.001–0.002 SG / ±0.5 °P
  • Calibration: at 20 °C
  • Price: €5–15
✅ Best for: tracking fermentation
🍬

Saccharometer (% Sugar)

A hydrometer with a sugar-percentage scale — the most intuitive for a distiller.

  • Range: 0–25% or 0–40%
  • Accurate before fermentation
  • During/after: reads low due to ethanol
  • Price: €5–10
⚠️ Best for: initial syrup measurement only
🔬

Refractometer

Measures the refractive index. Only 1–2 drops needed, fast reading.

  • Great for wort before fermentation
  • Ethanol distorts readings
  • Cloudy wash blurs the scale
  • Price: €15–50 (analog), €50–150 (digital)
🔵 Best for: initial wort check
📟

Electronic Density Meter

Digital device using an oscillating tube or ultrasonic sensor.

  • High accuracy, auto temperature compensation
  • Price: €100–500
  • Fragile, needs calibration

Chapter 3. Scales: Specific Gravity, Plato, Brix, Balling

3.1. Specific Gravity (SG)

Specific gravity is the ratio of liquid density to the density of pure water at the same temperature. It is dimensionless:

  • Pure water: 1.000
  • 20% sugar solution: ~1.083
  • Fully fermented wash: 0.990–1.005 (alcohol is lighter than water)
  • Range for distilling: 0.980–1.150

Specific gravity is the most common scale in the English-speaking tradition and among distillers using imported equipment.

3.2. Degrees Plato (°P)

Degrees Plato show the mass fraction of sucrose in solution as a percentage. 1 °P = 1 g of sucrose per 100 g of solution.

  • 0 °P = pure water
  • 10 °P = 10% sucrose by mass
  • 20 °P = 20% sucrose by mass

The Plato scale is convenient because it directly shows sugar concentration without conversion. In European practice (Germany, Austria, Czech Republic, Poland) this is the standard scale for brewing and winemaking.

3.3. Degrees Brix (°Bx)

Degrees Brix also show the mass fraction of sucrose. 1 °Bx ≈ 1 °P (the difference is less than 0.1% in the 0–25% range). The Brix scale has historically been used in winemaking and juice production.

For a distiller's practical purposes, °Bx and °P are interchangeable. If your refractometer is graduated in °Bx and a conversion table uses °P, the difference is negligible.

3.4. Degrees Balling (°Balling)

The Balling scale is a predecessor of the Plato scale, used in Central Europe in the 19th century. 1 °Balling ≈ 1 °P. It is rarely encountered in modern practice.

  • For intuitive understanding: °Plato. Sugar concentration is immediately visible.
  • For sugar syrup: % sugar (saccharometer). Easiest for dosing.
Recommendation: buy a dual-scale hydrometer (SG + °Plato or % sugar) and work with both.

Chapter 4. Measurement Practice: Step-by-Step Instructions

4.1. Equipment

To measure density you will need:

  • A hydrometer (saccharometer) with a suitable range.
  • A graduated cylinder (trial cylinder) of 200–250 ml, 25–30 cm tall. Its diameter should be 2–3 cm wider than the hydrometer.
  • A thermometer (if the hydrometer has no built-in temperature correction).
  • A clean cloth or paper towel for wiping the hydrometer.

4.2. Step-by-Step Procedure

1

Take a Sample

  • Open the fermentation vessel.
  • With a clean ladle, tube, or siphon, take 200–250 ml of wash from the middle of the vessel (not from the surface, not from the bottom).
  • If the wash is cloudy (grain, fruit), let the sample settle 1–2 minutes.
  • Pour the sample into the graduated cylinder.

Important: do not pour the sample back into the fermenter — it may have picked up airborne microorganisms. Pour it out.

2

Immerse the Hydrometer

  • Gently lower the hydrometer wide-end down (weight at the bottom).
  • Do not drop it — the glass is fragile and may crack on impact with the cylinder bottom.
  • Give it a slight spin to remove air bubbles clinging to the glass (bubbles distort the reading high).
  • Wait 5–10 seconds until it stabilizes.
3

Read the Value

  • Hold the cylinder at eye level.
  • Read at the bottom of the meniscus (the curved liquid surface).
  • The hydrometer must not touch the cylinder walls — if it does, spin it slightly.
  • Record the value to 0.001 (SG) or 0.5 °P.
4

Temperature Correction

Most hydrometers are calibrated at 20 °C. If the sample temperature differs, correct the reading:

  • 10 °C → −0.001 SG (−0.3 °P)
  • 15 °C → −0.0005 SG (−0.15 °P)
  • 20 °C → 0
  • 25 °C → +0.001 SG (+0.3 °P)
  • 30 °C → +0.002 SG (+0.5 °P)
  • 35 °C → +0.003 SG (+0.8 °P)

Recommendation: for simplicity, bring the sample to 20 °C (place the cylinder in cold or warm water) and measure without correction — this is more accurate than a calculated correction.

5

Record the Data

Keep a fermentation journal — a notebook or spreadsheet recording date, time, density (SG), density (°P), temperature, and notes. Such a journal lets you track dynamics, determine completion, spot problems, calculate actual strength, and build statistics for future batches.

4.3. Measurement Frequency

  • Before fermentation: 1 time (initial density).
  • During active fermentation (days 1–3): can skip, or measure once for control.
  • During slowing (days 4–7): once per day.
  • When completion is suspected: twice with a 24-hour interval (to confirm stability).
  • Before distillation: final reading.
Do not measure too often. Every opening of the vessel is contact with air and a risk of infection. 1–2 readings per day is the optimal frequency.

Chapter 5. Calculating Potential and Actual Alcohol

5.1. Potential Alcohol from Initial Density

Knowing the initial density (OG — Original Gravity), you can calculate the maximum possible alcohol content if all sugar is fermented.

🧮 Potential Alcohol by Specific Gravity
Potential ABV (%) = (OG − 1.000) × 131.25

Examples: OG 1.060 → 7.9%; OG 1.080 → 10.5%; OG 1.100 → 13.1% ABV.

🧮 Potential Alcohol by Degrees Plato
Potential ABV (%) ≈ °P × 0.55

Examples: 15 °P → 8.3%; 20 °P → 11.0%; 25 °P → 13.8% ABV.

These formulas give a theoretical maximum. The actual yield is always 5–10% lower due to yeast metabolism, glycerol formation, and incomplete fermentation.

5.2. Actual Alcohol from Initial and Final Density

Knowing the initial (OG) and final (FG — Final Gravity) densities, you can calculate the actual alcohol content of the wash.

🧮 Simple Formula
ABV (%) = (OG − FG) × 131.25

Example: OG 1.085, FG 1.000 → (1.085 − 1.000) × 131.25 = 11.2% ABV.

🧮 Accurate Formula (for OG above 1.060)
ABV = (76.08 × (OG − FG)) / (1.775 − OG) × (FG / 0.794)

Example: OG 1.085, FG 0.998 → 12.06% ABV. The difference from the simple formula at density 1.085 is ~0.5–1%, significant for precise planning.

5.3. Calculation by Degrees Plato

🧮 Plato Formula
ABV (%) ≈ (OG°P − FG°P) × 0.55

Example: OG 20 °P, FG 1 °P → (20 − 1) × 0.55 = 10.5% ABV. Simpler but less accurate at high densities; ±0.5% ABV error is acceptable for home use.

5.4. Calculating Potential Absolute Alcohol Yield

For distillation planning, it is useful to know how much absolute alcohol you will get from the wash.

🧮 Absolute Alcohol
Abs. alcohol (ml) = Wash volume (L) × Actual ABV (%) / 100

Example: 25 L of wash at 12% ABV → 25 × 12 / 100 = 3 liters of absolute alcohol.

🧮 Expected Distillate Volume
Distillate volume (ml) ≈ Abs. alcohol / Target ABV × 100

Example: 3 L abs. alcohol at target 42% → 3000 / 42 × 100 ≈ 7100 ml (7.1 L). With 10–15% losses: ~6.0–6.4 L of finished product.

Chapter 6. Degree of Fermentation and Process Control

6.1. Apparent Degree of Fermentation

The apparent degree of fermentation (attenuation) shows what fraction of the initial sugar was fermented by the yeast.

🧮 Attenuation
Attenuation (%) = (OG − FG) / (OG − 1.000) × 100

Example (complete): OG 1.085, FG 1.000 → 100%.
Example (incomplete): OG 1.085, FG 1.020 → 76.5%.

Wash Type Typical Attenuation Why
Sugar95–100%Pure sugar, fully fermentable
Grain (wheat, rye)70–85%Unfermentable dextrins
Grain (with glucoamylase)85–95%Enzymes break down dextrins
Fruit (apples, grapes)85–95%Some sugars and acids not fermentable
Potato80–90%Pectins, residual starch
Honey75–90%Complex sugar profile

6.2. Interpreting the Degree of Fermentation

  • Sugar wash: if attenuation is below 90%, fermentation stopped prematurely. Look for the cause (temperature, nutrients, yeast).
  • Grain wash: 70–80% is normal. Unfermentable dextrins are a natural part of grain wort.
  • Fruit wash: 80–90% is normal. Some acids and pectins are not fermentable.

Diagnostic signs from the curve:

  • Sharp drop in the first 2 days, then a plateau: fermentation stopped. Check temperature and nutrients.
  • Smooth, even decrease: normal fermentation.
  • Density unchanged for 48 hours: fermentation complete (if 0–2 °P) or stalled (if above 4 °P).
  • Density increased: measurement error or infection (lactic acid bacteria can temporarily raise density).

6.4. Determining the Moment of Fermentation Completion

The readiness criterion is density stability:

  1. Measure the density.
  2. Wait 24 hours.
  3. Measure again.
  4. If the readings match (within ±0.5 °P or ±0.002 SG) and the value is in the expected range (0–2 °P for sugar, 2–5 °P for grain) — fermentation is complete.
Do not base a decision on a single reading. A single value may be accidental. Two matching readings 24 hours apart are a reliable criterion.

Chapter 7. Measurement Specifics by Wash Type

🍬 Sugar Wash

Initial: 1.060–1.100 (15–24 °P)

  • Easy to measure — clear or slightly cloudy.
  • For very dense syrup (>1.120), dilute sample 1:1 and multiply by 2.

🌾 Grain Wash

Initial: 1.040–1.070 (10–17 °P)

  • Cloudy — let sample settle 5–10 min or strain.
  • Final density usually 2–5 °P (dextrins) — this is normal.
  • Rely on reading stability, not absolute value.

🍎 Fruit Wash

Initial: 1.035–1.070 (9–17 °P)

  • Strain sample through cheesecloth or fine sieve.
  • Final density 1–4 °P due to acids and pectins.
  • Use two readings 24 h apart to confirm completion.

🍯 Honey Wash

Initial: 1.060–1.120 (15–28 °P)

  • Can be cloudy and foamy — let settle, remove foam.
  • Ferments slowly — measure every 2–3 days.
  • Final density: 1–4 °P.

🥔 Potato Wash

Initial: 1.040–1.070 (10–17 °P)

  • Very thick and cloudy — strain through cheesecloth.
  • Make sure the hydrometer floats freely and doesn't stick.

Chapter 9. Instrument Care and Storage

9.1. Cleaning After Use

After each measurement:

  • Rinse the hydrometer with warm water.
  • Wipe with a soft cloth or paper towel.
  • Do not use abrasive materials — they scratch the glass and distort the scale.
  • Make sure no liquid remains in the weighted bulb.

9.2. Storage

  • Store the hydrometer in a rigid tube or soft case to protect from impacts.
  • Do not store it in or near the fermenter — an accidental drop will break the glass.
  • Protect from sharp temperature changes.

9.3. Calibration

To check the hydrometer's accuracy:

  1. Fill the cylinder with distilled water at 20 °C.
  2. Lower the hydrometer.
  3. The reading should be 1.000 (or 0 °P).
  4. If the deviation exceeds ±0.002, the instrument is inaccurate and needs replacement.

Sugar solution check: dissolve 100 g of sugar in 900 ml of water (10% solution), bring to 20 °C. The reading should be ~1.040 or ~10 °P.

9.4. Service Life

A glass hydrometer, used carefully, lasts 10–20 years. Main reasons for replacement:

  • Crack or chip in the glass.
  • Clouded scale.
  • Shifted internal weight.
  • Inaccuracy during calibration.

Chapter 10. Advanced Techniques: Refractometer and Alcohol Correction

10.1. Using a Refractometer Before Fermentation

A refractometer is ideal for measuring initial density before fermentation begins, when there is no ethanol in the wort. Drop 1–2 drops on the prism, close the lid, read the value.

  • For sugar syrup: accuracy ±0.2 °Bx.
  • For grain wort: strain through cheesecloth to remove suspended matter.
  • For fruit juice: squeeze the juice, drop it on the prism.

10.2. Refractometer Correction During Fermentation

If you want to use a refractometer during fermentation (when ethanol is already present), the readings need correction. Ethanol lowers the refractive index, and the device shows a low density.

Recommendation: use a hydrometer for tracking fermentation. A refractometer is convenient for initial readings and quick wort checks, but not for accurately determining final density. Use online correction tables if needed.

10.3. Electronic Density Meters and Automation

For those who want maximum accuracy and convenience:

  • Digital refractometer (€50–150): automatic temperature compensation, display, measurement memory.
  • Electronic hydrometer (€100–300): immersion sensor, smartphone connection, fermentation graphs.
  • Automatic monitoring systems (€200–500): density and temperature sensors, online monitoring, notifications.

For home distilling these are overkill, but for craft distilleries and serious enthusiasts they save time and improve reproducibility.

Chapter 11. Practical Calculation Examples

📋 Example 1: Sugar Wash, 25 Liters

Given: OG 1.085 (20.5 °P), FG 1.000 (0 °P), volume 25 L.
Actual ABV: (1.085 − 1.000) × 131.25 = 11.2% ABV
Attenuation: (1.085 − 1.000) / (1.085 − 1.000) × 100 = 100%
Absolute alcohol: 25 × 11.2 / 100 = 2.8 L
Expected distillate (42%): 2800 / 42 × 100 ≈ 6700 ml. With 12% losses: ~5900 ml

📋 Example 2: Grain Wash, 30 Liters

Given: OG 1.055 (13.5 °P), FG 1.012 (3 °P), volume 30 L.
Actual ABV: (1.055 − 1.012) × 131.25 = 5.6% ABV
Attenuation: (1.055 − 1.012) / (1.055 − 1.000) × 100 = 78% (normal for grain — unfermentable dextrins)
Absolute alcohol: 30 × 5.6 / 100 = 1.68 L
Expected distillate (45%): 1680 / 45 × 100 ≈ 3700 ml. With 15% losses: ~3100 ml

📋 Example 3: Honey Wash, 20 Liters

Given: OG 1.095 (22.6 °P), FG 1.005 (1.2 °P), volume 20 L.
Actual ABV: (1.095 − 1.005) × 131.25 = 11.8% ABV
Attenuation: (1.095 − 1.005) / (1.095 − 1.000) × 100 = 94.7%
Absolute alcohol: 20 × 11.8 / 100 = 2.36 L

Chapter 12. Frequently Asked Questions

Q Can I measure density during active fermentation?

Yes, but the readings will be unstable due to dissolved CO₂ and suspended yeast. For an accurate reading, let the sample settle 5–10 minutes and remove bubbles. But in the active phase, measurement is not mandatory — just observe the dynamics.

Q My hydrometer shows 1.000 but the wash tastes sweet. Why?

Ethanol lowers density below 1.000. If the wash has residual sugar and ethanol at the same time, density can be 1.000 or lower while sweetness remains. Measure density twice with a 24-hour interval. If it is stable, fermentation is complete and residual sugar is minimal.

Q Wash density is below 1.000. Is this normal?

Yes. Fully fermented wash has a density of 0.990–1.005 because ethanol is lighter than water. This is a sign of successful fermentation completion.

Q Can I use one hydrometer for all wash types?

Yes, if the instrument's range covers your densities (0.980–1.150). For very dense syrups (>1.120) you may need an extended-range hydrometer.

Q How do I measure the density of a thick grain wash?

Strain the sample through a fine sieve or cheesecloth and let it settle 5–10 minutes. Measure in the clear liquid. Suspended particles distort the reading.

Q Refractometer or hydrometer — which is better?

For home distilling, the hydrometer is the better choice. It is cheaper, more accurate in fermenting wash, and does not require ethanol correction. A refractometer is convenient for initial wort readings but not for tracking fermentation.

Q How often should I calibrate a hydrometer?

A glass hydrometer does not require calibration, but you should check accuracy 1–2 times a year (distilled water at 20 °C = 1.000). If the reading deviates more than ±0.002, replace the instrument.

Q Can I calculate wash strength without a hydrometer?

Approximately — yes, by the amount of sugar added and the volume. But without measuring initial and final density, accuracy will be ±2–3%, and you won't know whether the sugar is fully fermented. A €10 hydrometer pays for itself on the very first run.

Conclusion: From Intuition to Precision

The saccharometer and hydrometer are the simplest and most important instruments in a distiller's arsenal. A €10 glass tube turns guessing into measurement, intuition into calculation, and chance into reproducibility.

Three Rules to Remember

📏 Measure Twice One reading is information. Two matching readings 24 hours apart are a diagnosis. Do not make decisions based on a single value.
📓 Record Your Data A fermentation journal with initial and final density, dates, and temperatures is your knowledge base. After 10 batches you will know exactly how your wash behaves and plan results within ±5%.
🧮 Calculate Before Distilling, Not After Knowing initial density and wash volume, you can predict the expected alcohol yield before turning on the boiler. This lets you plan time, receiving vessels, and the number of runs.

Density is the language your wash speaks to you. Learn to understand it — and the process stops being a lottery. Every measurement is an answer to the question "what's happening?", every calculation is a prediction of what comes next.

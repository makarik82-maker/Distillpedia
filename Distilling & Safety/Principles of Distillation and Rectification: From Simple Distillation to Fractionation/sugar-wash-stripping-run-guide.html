Introduction: The First Run Is the Foundation of the Whole Process

The first distillation of sugar wash is the stage where a 10–14% ABV liquid turns into a 25–40% ABV raw spirit. At this stage, no final product is created. Raw spirit is a semi-finished product, a concentrate that will be purified and separated into fractions during the second, fractional distillation.

But it is precisely the first run that determines how much alcohol you will get and in what condition it will enter the second stage. Stopping collection too early means losing yield. Stopping too late means fusel oils and furfural migrating into the raw spirit. Overheating means scorched yeast and a burnt off-taste. Skipping preparation means foaming and wash eruption.

Sugar wash is the simplest type of wash to distill. It contains no grain proteins, fruit pectins, or honey waxes. But the simplicity of the raw material does not mean the process can be done carelessly. Sugar wash contains yeast sediment, residual metabolic products, and volatile impurities that, with improper heating, migrate into the distillate and complicate the second distillation.

This article is a step-by-step guide to the first distillation of sugar wash. Every step is numbered, every parameter specified, every mistake explained. Follow the instructions — and you will get a clean, stable raw spirit ready for fractional distillation.

Chapter 1. The Purpose of the First Run: What We Do and What We Don't

1.1. The Task of the First Run

The first run solves one task: extract the maximum ethanol from the wash and concentrate it into a smaller volume.

  • Input: 20 liters of wash at 12% ABV (2.4 liters of absolute alcohol).
  • Output: ~5–6 liters of raw spirit at 35–40% ABV (the same 2.4 liters of absolute alcohol in a smaller volume).
  • What remains in the still: water, dead yeast, proteins, minerals, non-volatile organic substances, glycerol, part of the heavy fusel oils.

1.2. What the First Run Does NOT Do

  • Does not separate fractions. Heads, hearts, and tails are collected into a single container. Separation happens during the second run.
  • Does not purify the spirit. Raw spirit contains methanol, acetaldehyde, and fusel oils. It must not be drunk.
  • Does not create aroma. For sugar wash, aroma is not a goal. The goal is maximum yield of neutral spirit.

1.3. Why Not Collect Fractions During the First Run

Some distillers collect heads during the first run too. This is permissible but not necessary. The reasons:

  • During the second run, heads will be collected again. Double head collection leads to ethanol loss, since some ethanol leaves with the head fraction.
  • The first run is about concentration. The more distillate collected, the higher the overall yield.
  • Raw spirit is not the final product. All impurities will be removed during the second run.
Recommendation: during the first distillation of sugar wash, collect all distillate into a single container. The exception is if the wash has a pronounced off-odor (acetone, hydrogen sulfide): in that case, collect the first 50–100 ml separately.

A
Wash Preparation
Steps 1–3
1
Confirm fermentation is complete
  • Hydrometer reading: 0–2 °P (0.998–1.008 SG).
  • Taste: dry, slightly bitter, no sweetness.
  • Two readings 24 hours apart give the same result.
If the wash is under-fermented (gravity above 3 °P), the first run will lose yield: unfermented sugar will remain in the still and caramelize when heated.
2
Clarify the wash
  • Let the wash settle for 24–48 hours at 10–15 °C. The yeast will sink to the bottom.
  • Carefully decant the clarified wash off the sediment using a siphon or by tilting the container.
  • Do not pour the residue with sediment (0.5–1 liter) into the still.
Why: yeast sediment scorches on the bottom of the still when heated, creating a burnt smell and making cleanup difficult. Clarification also reduces foaming.
3
Degas the wash (optional)

Stir the clarified wash with a clean paddle for 2–3 minutes, or pour it from one container to another in a thin stream. This removes dissolved CO₂ and reduces foaming during heating.

B
Apparatus Preparation
Steps 4–7
4
Rinse the apparatus
  • Run hot water (50–60 °C) through the still and vapor outlet for 5 minutes.
  • Verify no residue from previous runs remains in the still.
  • Rinse the condenser with water.
5
Check for leaks
  • Assemble the apparatus completely.
  • Close the condenser outlet (with a finger or a plug, for example).
  • Blow gently into the vapor outlet or create slight pressure.
  • If air escapes from the joints, tighten the clamps and replace the seal.
6
Connect water to the condenser
  • Supply hose to the lower condenser fitting.
  • Drain hose from the upper fitting.
  • Water should flow bottom-to-top to fully fill the condenser jacket.
  • Check the pressure: water should flow in a steady stream, without surges.
7
Prepare the receiving container
  • Glass jar or bottle of 5–10 liters.
  • Clean and dry.
  • Place it under the condenser outlet.
  • Make sure the distillate will drip/flow into the container, not past it.
C
Safety
Steps 8–11
8
Ensure ventilation

Ethanol vapors are flammable. Distill in a well-ventilated room. Do not use open flames near the apparatus (for gas heating, use a burner with a long hose and keep the cylinder at a distance).

9
Remove flammable items

Within a 1-meter radius of the apparatus there should be no paper, fabrics, or solvents.

10
Prepare extinguishing means

Have a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO₂) or a thick blanket within reach.

11
Do not leave the apparatus unattended

The first run takes 2–4 hours. Stay in the room for the entire process.

Chapter 3. Start-Up and Heating: Step by Step

2
Start & Heat-Up
Steps 12–17
12
Fill the still
  • Pour the clarified wash into the still.
  • Fill no more than 2/3 of the still's volume. For a 30-liter still — maximum 20 liters of wash. For a 20-liter still — maximum 13 liters.
Why not more: when heated, the wash foams and increases in volume. If overfilled, foam can enter the vapor outlet and condenser, contaminating the distillate.
13
Install the lid and connect
  • Place the lid on the still. Tighten the clamps or bolts evenly, diagonally (like a wheel rim).
  • Verify the thermometer is installed and shows room temperature.
  • Connect the vapor outlet tube to the lid and to the condenser.
  • Make sure the receiving container sits under the condenser outlet.
14
Turn on the heat
  • Turn the heat to maximum power.
  • The goal of the first stage is to bring the wash to a boil as quickly as possible, to minimize the time the yeast spends in a warm environment.
15
Wait for boiling
  • The still temperature will begin to rise: 20 → 40 → 60 → 70 °C.
  • At 60–70 °C, weak vapor or condensate may come from the condenser. This is normal: highly volatile components (acetaldehyde, residual CO₂) are evaporating.
  • Do not collect this condensate into the main container. If you wish, place a separate small container for the first 5–10 minutes. But for sugar wash this is not critical.
16
Start of collection
  • When the still temperature reaches 78–82 °C, a steady stream of distillate will come from the condenser.
  • Reduce the heat to moderate (60–70% of maximum). From this point, heating should be stable, without surges.
  • Place the main receiving container under the stream.
  • Record the time collection started and the still temperature.
17
Set the collection rate
  • For the first run, the collection rate can be higher than for fractional distillation: 3–5 drops per second, or a thin stream 1–2 mm thick.
  • The goal is to extract the alcohol quickly, without spending time on fine separation.
  • Do not allow collection too fast (thick stream, 10+ drops/sec): the vapor won't have time to condense, part will bypass the condenser, and the raw spirit strength will drop.

Chapter 4. Collecting the Distillate: The Main Phase

3
Collection
Steps 18–21
18
Monitor the process

During collection, monitor three parameters:

  • Still temperature:
    • Start of collection: 78–82 °C.
    • Middle of collection: 85–92 °C.
    • End of collection: 95–99 °C.
    • Temperature should rise smoothly, without surges.
  • Stream strength:
    • Measure with the alcoholmeter every 300–500 ml of collected distillate.
    • Start of collection: 70–80% ABV.
    • Middle of collection: 50–60% ABV.
    • End of collection: 20–30% ABV.
    • Strength should decrease as collection progresses.
  • Distillate appearance:
    • Clear, colorless.
    • If the distillate is cloudy — wash splashes may be entering the vapor outlet. Reduce the heat.
    • If the distillate has a yellow or brown tint — scorching in the still. Immediately reduce the heat.
19
Collect the raw spirit
  • All distillate is collected into a single receiving container.
  • Do not separate into fractions during the first run (see Chapter 1).
  • Periodically measure strength and volume. Record in the journal.
20
Control foaming

In the first 10–20 minutes of collection, foaming is possible. If foam rises toward the vapor outlet:

  • Reduce the heat by 20–30%.
  • Wait for the foam to settle.
  • Smoothly return the heat to the working level.

If foam enters the vapor outlet and condenser:

  • Stop collection for 2–3 minutes.
  • Reduce the heat.
  • Rinse the receiving container if wash got into it.
21
Interim measurements

Every 30 minutes or every 500 ml of distillate:

  • Measure the stream strength.
  • Measure the still temperature.
  • Record the data in the journal.

Chapter 5. Completing the Run: When to Stop

4
Completion
Steps 22–26
22
Determine when to stop collection

Stop collection when any of the three criteria is reached:

  • Criterion 1: stream strength. Strength has dropped to 20–25% ABV. Below this threshold the distillate is dominated by water and heavy impurities. Further collection is pointless: you are collecting water with impurities, not alcohol.
  • Criterion 2: still temperature. Temperature has reached 97–99 °C. This means there is almost no alcohol left in the still and water is boiling.
  • Criterion 3: collection rate. Distillate is dripping less than 1 drop every 5–10 seconds. Further heating yields negligible distillate at high energy cost.
23
Stop collection
  • Move the receiving container out from under the stream.
  • Place a small container for the last drops (optional), or simply stop collecting.
  • Record the final volume and end time.
24
Turn off the heat
  • Turn off the still heat.
  • Do not turn off the condenser water supply for another 5–10 minutes. Hot vapor remains in the still and must condense.
  • After 5–10 minutes, when dripping from the condenser stops, turn off the water supply.
25
Cool the still
  • Let the still cool naturally for 30–60 minutes.
  • Do not open the lid of a hot still. Steam can burn your face and hands.
  • Do not cool the still with cold water: a sudden temperature change can deform the metal or damage the enamel.
26
Open and clean
  • When the still temperature has dropped below 50 °C, open the lid.
  • Pour the spent wash (the residue in the still) into a disposal container.
  • Wash the still with hot water and detergent.
  • Rinse the vapor outlet and condenser with water.
  • Wipe dry.
Disposal of spent wash: sugar wash spent wash can be poured down the drain (for small volumes, up to 10 liters). For large volumes, dilute with water 1:10 and use as garden fertilizer. Do not pour hot spent wash onto plants or into water bodies.

Chapter 6. Handling and Storing Raw Spirit

5
Processing & Storage
Steps 27–30
27
Measure strength and volume
  • Stir the raw spirit in the receiving container.
  • Take 100–250 ml in a graduated cylinder.
  • Measure strength with the alcoholmeter at 20 °C (or with temperature correction).
  • Measure the total volume of raw spirit.
  • Calculate the amount of absolute alcohol:
Absolute Alcohol
Absolute alcohol (ml) = Raw spirit volume (ml) × Strength (%) / 100

Example: 5,500 ml of raw spirit × 38% / 100 = 2,090 ml of absolute alcohol.

28
Evaluate the yield

Compare the actual yield with the theoretical:

Theoretical Yield
Theoretical yield = Wash volume (L) × Wash strength (%) / 100 × 1000

Example: 20 L × 12% / 100 × 1000 = 2,400 ml of absolute alcohol.

First-Run Efficiency
Efficiency = Actual yield / Theoretical yield × 100%

Example: 2,090 / 2,400 × 100% = 87%.
Standard: first-run efficiency is 80–90%. Below 75% indicates losses (incomplete collection, leaks, scorching).

29
Dilute before the second run
  • Dilute the raw spirit with clean water to 20–30% ABV.
  • Why: below 30% strength, fraction separation during the second run is clearer. Heads and tails separate more easily.
  • Use clean water (filtered, bottled, or settled).
  • Pour water into the raw spirit, not the other way around (for even mixing).
  • Stir.
Example: 5,500 ml of raw spirit at 38% ABV → dilute to 25% ABV:
Water volume = 5,500 × (38/25 − 1) = 5,500 × 0.52 = 2,860 ml of water.
Total: 5,500 + 2,860 = 8,360 ml at 25% ABV.
30
Store the raw spirit

If the second run is not done immediately:

  • Pour the diluted raw spirit into a glass container with a tight lid.
  • Store at 5–20 °C in a dark place.
  • Do not store in plastic containers longer than 2–3 days: ethanol dissolves plastic.
  • Shelf life: up to 2–3 months without quality loss.
  • Label the container: date, volume, strength, wash type.

Chapter 8. Specifics for Different Apparatus Types

🔥 8.1. Direct-Heat Apparatus (heating element, gas burner)

  • Risk of yeast sediment scorching on the heating element.
  • Wash clarification is critically important.
  • Heat: maximum until boiling, then 60–70%.
  • Stir the wash during the first 10 minutes of heating (if there is a stirrer) to prevent scorching.

♨️ 8.2. Steam-Jacket Apparatus (water bath)

  • No scorching risk: the wash does not contact the heater.
  • Heating is slower: time to boiling increases by 15–30 minutes.
  • You can distill unclarified wash (but clarification is still desirable).

💨 8.3. Steam-Generator Apparatus

  • Steam is fed into the wash from a separate generator.
  • No scorching.
  • Risk of diluting the wash with condensate: in the first minutes of collection the strength may be lower than expected.
  • Clarification is not critical, but desirable.

🫙 8.4. Apparatus with a Thump Keg

  • The thump keg catches splashes and part of the heavy fractions.
  • Raw spirit strength is 3–5% higher than without a thump keg.
  • Rinse the thump keg after each run.

❄️ 8.5. Apparatus with a Dephlegmator

  • The dephlegmator creates partial reflux return.
  • Raw spirit strength is 5–10% higher than without a dephlegmator.
  • For the first run, the dephlegmator can be turned off or set to minimum: the goal is maximum yield, not purity.

Chapter 9. Yield Calculation and Loss Control

9.1. Theoretical Yield

From 1 kg of sugar, you theoretically get 510 ml of absolute alcohol. For wash with 4 kg of sugar per 20 liters of water:

Theoretical yield = 4 × 510 = 2,040 ml of absolute alcohol

9.2. Practical Yield

The practical yield is always lower than the theoretical due to:

  • Incomplete fermentation (losses 2–5%).
  • Alcohol remaining in the spent wash (losses 5–10%).
  • Evaporation and leaks (losses 1–3%).
  • Absorption by yeast (losses 1–2%).
Normal practical yield: 80–90% of theoretical.

9.3. Alcohol Balance

After the first run, perform a balance:

Parameter Value
Theoretical yield (by sugar) 2,040 ml abs.
Actual yield in wash (by gravity) 1,950 ml abs.
Yield in raw spirit 1,750 ml abs.
Distillation losses 200 ml abs. (10%)
Efficiency 86%
If efficiency is below 75%, look for the cause: leaks, incomplete collection, or scorching.

Chapter 10. Frequently Asked Questions

Q Do I need to collect heads during the first run?

For sugar wash — not necessary. Heads will be collected during the second, fractional run. If the wash has a pronounced off-odor (acetone, hydrogen sulfide), collect the first 50–100 ml into a separate container. In all other cases, collect all distillate into one container.

Q Can I distill wash without clarifying it?

Technically yes, but not recommended. Yeast sediment scorches on the bottom of the still, creates a burnt smell, and makes cleanup difficult. Clarification for 24–48 hours is a simple procedure that greatly improves the process.

Q The raw spirit came out cloudy. Is this normal?

Yes. Cloudiness is caused by tiny wash droplets entering the vapor (splashes) or by dissolved substances precipitating during cooling. During the second run the cloudiness will disappear. If the cloudiness is strong, reduce the heat on the next run.

Q The raw spirit strength came out at 25%. Is that low?

Raw spirit strength on the first run depends on when you stop collection. If you collected down to 20–25% in the stream, average raw spirit strength will be 30–40%. If you stopped earlier (at 30–35% in the stream), strength will be higher (40–45%), but yield will be lower. A strength of 25% means you collected for a very long time. This is not a mistake, but on the second run you will have to work with a larger volume.

Q Can I drink the raw spirit?

No. Raw spirit contains methanol, acetaldehyde, fusel oils, and other toxic impurities. You can drink only after the second, fractional distillation with fraction separation.

Q How long does the first run of 20 liters of wash take?

2–4 hours depending on heating power and apparatus design. Heating to boiling: 20–40 minutes. Distillate collection: 1.5–3 hours. Cooling: 30–60 minutes.

Q Do I need to dilute raw spirit before the second run?

Recommended. Dilution to 20–30% ABV improves fraction separation during fractional distillation. If the raw spirit strength is below 30%, dilution is not required.

Q Can I collect the spent wash and distill it again?

No. The spent wash retains 0.5–3% alcohol and non-volatile impurities. Re-distillation is pointless: you will spend energy heating water and get a negligible amount of low-quality distillate.

Q What do I do if the wash scorched in the still?

Immediately turn off the heat. Let the still cool. Pour out the spent wash. Wash the still with hot water and baking soda (2 tablespoons per liter). If scorching is severe, soak for 2–3 hours. Distillate collected before the scorching can be used. Distillate with a burnt smell — re-distill or dispose of.

Q Can I distill several batches of wash in a row?

Yes, but between batches wash the still with hot water. Residue from the previous wash can scorch when heating the new batch.

Q How do I know the condenser is working properly?

The distillate at the condenser outlet should feel cold or cool (below 30 °C). If the distillate is warm or hot, increase the water supply. The water exiting the condenser should be warm (30–50 °C), but not hot.

Q What do I do if vapor, not liquid, comes from the condenser?

The heating power exceeds the condenser's capacity. Reduce the heat by 20–30%. Increase the water supply to the condenser. If the problem persists, replace the condenser with a higher-capacity one.

Conclusion: The First Run Is Extraction

The first distillation of sugar wash is not the creation of a beverage. It is the extraction of alcohol from a liquid. The task is simple: heat, evaporate, condense, collect. But the simplicity of the task does not mean it can be done carelessly. Every skipped step — clarification, temperature control, timely stopping of collection — turns into yield loss, distillate contamination, or a scorched still.

Three Rules to Remember

Clarify the wash. 24–48 hours of settling and decanting off the sediment prevent scorching, foaming, and burnt smell. This is the simplest step that gives the biggest effect.
Collect down to 20–25% in the stream. Don't stop early — you'll lose 10–15% of alcohol. Don't continue below 15% — you'll be collecting water with impurities. Stream strength and still temperature (97–99 °C) are the two stopping criteria.
Raw spirit is not the final product. It must not be drunk. It must not be stored in plastic. Dilute to 20–30%, pour into glass, and prepare for the second, fractional distillation. That is where the quality is created.

The first run is the foundation. Even, clean, complete. Everything built on top depends on how you lay it.

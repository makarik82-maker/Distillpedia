Introduction: The Vanguard of Distillate

In the home distilling community, a dangerous myth still lives: "The first drops of moonshine are the strongest — you should drink them yourself or treat a respected guest." This tradition, rooted in village moonshining of the last century, is a direct road to severe poisoning, blindness, and in the worst case, death.

The first milliliters coming out of the still's condenser are called head fractions (or simply "heads"). This is not just "strong alcohol." This is a concentrate of the most volatile, aggressive, and toxic compounds formed during fermentation. The distiller's task is not just to separate them, but to do it surgically precisely — without losing the valuable "body" (drinkable ethanol) and without leaving poison in the final product.

In this article, we'll break heads down to molecules, understand where they come from, how to recognize them without complex laboratory equipment, and how to ensure the absolute safety of your drink.

Chapter 1. Chemical Portrait of "Heads": Who Is Who?

Head fractions (in professional terminology — the ether-aldehyde fraction, EAF) are a complex mixture of low-boiling substances. Their boiling point is lower than or close to ethanol's boiling point (78.3 °C), so when the still is heated, they rush into the vapor line first.

Let's examine the main "characters" of this toxic cocktail.

☠️ Methanol CH₃OH

Boiling point64.7 °C
Smell & tasteIndistinguishable from ethanol
SourcePectin breakdown (not yeast metabolism)
Deadly poison. Metabolized into formaldehyde and formic acid, which destroy the optic nerve and cause severe metabolic acidosis. Lethal dose: 30–100 ml.

⚠️ Acetaldehyde CH₃CHO

Boiling point20.8 °C
SmellGreen apples, cut grass, solvent
SourceYeast metabolism intermediate
Main culprit of severe hangover. 10–30× more toxic than ethanol. Binds to proteins and DNA, causes tachycardia, nausea, facial flushing. Proven carcinogen.

🧪 Ethyl Acetate CH₃COOCH₂CH₃

Boiling point77.1 °C
SmellNail polish remover, glue
SourceEthanol + acetic acid reaction
Irritates respiratory mucous membranes and eyes. In large doses, causes CNS depression, nausea, dizziness.

💨 Acetone CH₃COCH₃

Boiling point56.0 °C
SmellSweetish-chemical, paint solvent
SourceYeast stress (nitrogen deficit, high temp)
Toxic to liver and kidneys, causes mucous membrane irritation.
DEBUNKING THE MAIN MYTH

"Methanol Boils First — Just Pour Out the First 50 ml"

Many beginners think that since methanol boils at 64 °C and ethanol at 78 °C, it's enough to simply pour out the first 50 ml of distillate and the methanol will be gone. This is a fundamental error.

Distillation is not just boiling of pure substances — it's evaporation of a complex multicomponent mixture where substances form azeotropes (inseparable mixtures) and carry each other along. Methanol dissolves excellently in water and ethanol, and its vapors exit the still throughout the entire distillation, not just in the first minutes.

However, at the very beginning of the process (in the heads) its concentration relative to liquid volume is maximal, and it comes out together with aldehydes and ethers. Therefore, heads must be collected not "by time" but by volume and organoleptics.

Chapter 2. Physiology: Why You Can't Drink Heads

If you accidentally didn't separate heads and drank such distillate, your body will face a cascade of toxic reactions:

  • Nervous system strike: Acetone and acetaldehyde quickly penetrate the blood-brain barrier, causing sharp constriction of brain vessels, spasms, and severe migraine.
  • Liver collapse: The enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) in the liver rushes to process the poisons. But methanol is processed by this same enzyme into formaldehyde, which instantly kills liver cells (hepatocytes).
  • Blindness: Formic acid (methanol's breakdown product) selectively accumulates in the vitreous body of the eye and optic nerve, causing its edema and irreversible atrophy.
  • Severe hangover: Even if there was little methanol, a shock dose of acetaldehyde and ethyl acetate guarantees tremor, tachycardia, panic attacks, and multi-day intoxication.
Conclusion: Separating heads is not a question of drink taste. It's a question of biological safety.

Chapter 3. The Math of Distillation: How Much to Collect?

There is no universal figure of "pour out the first 100 ml." The volume of heads directly depends on the amount of absolute alcohol (AA) in your still and the type of raw material.

3.1 Calculation by Absolute Alcohol (AA)

This is the most precise and professional method.

AA = Raw spirit volume (L) × Raw spirit strength (%) / 100
Example: You have 10 liters of raw spirit at 30% strength in the still.
AA = 10 × 30 / 100 = 3 liters (3000 ml) of absolute alcohol.

Collection rules:

  • For sugar mash: Collect 5–8% of AA. (3000 ml × 0.05 = 150 ml of heads).
  • For grain mash: Collect 8–10% of AA (grain gives more aldehydes). (3000 ml × 0.10 = 300 ml).
  • For fruit/berry/potato mash: Collect 10–15% of AA due to high methanol and complex ester risk. (3000 ml × 0.12 = 360 ml).

3.2 Calculation by Sugar (for First Distillation)

If you're distilling mash for the first time and don't know the exact strength, you can calculate from the amount of sugar added to the mash.

Collect 60–100 ml of heads per 1 kg of sugar added to the mash
Example: The mash had 5 kg of sugar. Collect 300 to 500 ml of heads.

Chapter 4. Practice of Collection: Surgery of Aroma

Collecting heads does not tolerate haste. This is the slowest stage of distillation.

⏱️ 4.1 Collection Speed

Heads must be collected drop by drop (1–3 drops per second).

Why? If you open the tap strongly, vapor will fly through the column or thump keg too quickly, and heavy impurities won't have time to condense and return to the still (as phlegm). They'll fly into the receiving container together with good alcohol. Slow collection ensures maximum vapor-phlegm contact and clear fraction separation.

👃 4.2 Organoleptic Control (Nose and Skin)

Math gives a guideline, but the final decision is always made by the distiller's nose.

Rub test (Gold standard):

  1. Drop 2–3 drops of distillate on a clean palm.
  2. Rub the drops with the second palm until completely dry (alcohol and water evaporate in seconds).
  3. Smell the skin.
  • If it smells of acetone, solvent, chemicals, green apple — heads are still coming. Continue collecting.
  • If the smell became clean, alcoholic, sweetish or neutral — heads are finished, "body" has started.

Smell from the glass: Bring a glass with distillate to your nose, but don't inhale deeply (aldehyde vapors can cause respiratory spasm). Take short, careful breaths from the side of the glass. Sharpness, burning in the nose, and tearing are sure signs of heads.

🥃 4.3 Fractional Collection (Small Containers Method)

Experienced distillers never pour heads into one large jar. They substitute small glasses (30–50 ml each).

Why? Because the boundary between heads and body is blurred. The second-to-last glass may already have clean alcohol, and the last "head" glass may have a transitional fraction. In the morning, with a fresh head, you can smell each glass and return clean alcohol to the "body," and pour out the dirty one. This saves up to 10–15% of product.

Chapter 5. Strategy: First and Second Distillation

How to work with heads at different stages?

First Distillation (Obtaining Raw Spirit)

Goal: Extract all alcohol from the mash as quickly as possible.

Action: Many professionals don't collect heads at all during the first distillation (especially for sugar mash) so as not to lose ethanol that is "smeared" throughout the entire run. They pour everything into one container.

Exception: If the mash is fruit or grain, you can collect the first 50–100 ml (the so-called "pre-head" or foreshots) to remove the most aggressive acetone fraction and protect the copper parts of the still from corrosion.

Second Distillation (Fractional Distillation of Raw Spirit)

Goal: Purification and obtaining a drinkable product.

Preparation: Raw spirit must be diluted with water to 15–25%. In a low-alcohol environment, the rectification coefficient (separation) works much better, and heads separate much more clearly.

Action: This is where strict mathematical calculation and slow drop-by-drop collection with organoleptic control are applied.

Chapter 6. Utilization and Storage: Where to Put the Poison?

Collected heads are toxic waste. They must be handled accordingly.

6.1 What You CANNOT Do:

  • Drink or use for tinctures. No "medicinal" rubs with heads. Toxins will penetrate through the skin and cause poisoning.
  • Pour into sewage in large volumes. Methanol and acetaldehyde vapors are explosive and toxic to bacteria in septic tanks and treatment facilities.
  • Store in plastic bottles from water/juice. Someone in the family may accidentally drink it. This is a frequent cause of tragedies.
  • Add to the next mash. Some "thrifty" moonshiners pour heads into a new mash to "not lose alcohol." This leads to poison accumulation (cumulative effect) and spoilage of the next batch's taste.

6.2 How to Store Properly:

  • Container: Only glass or special chemically resistant plastic (HDPE).
  • Labeling: Large, bright inscription "POISON! HEADS! DO NOT DRINK!"
  • Location: Closed cabinet, inaccessible to children and animals.

6.3 Useful Applications (Technical Needs):

Heads are an excellent solvent and fuel. They can be used for:

  • Degreasing surfaces before painting or gluing.
  • Cleaning tools (brushes, soldering irons, metal parts).
  • Kindling: Impregnating wood chips for lighting a grill or fireplace (they burn excellently, without soot).
  • Winter washer fluid: In extreme cases, diluted with water, can be used for wiping car headlights and windows (but don't pour into the washer reservoir regularly due to aggressiveness to rubber and toxicity of vapors in the cabin).
  • Fuel: For alcohol burners, tourist stoves, or biofireplaces.

Chapter 7. Raw Materials Specifics: Where Are the Most Heads?

Not all mashes are equally dangerous. Understanding the raw material helps adjust the collection volume.

Raw Material Type Head Fraction Level Specifics & Recommendations
Sugar mash Low / Medium Much acetaldehyde and ethyl acetate. Almost no methanol. Collect 5–7% of AA.
Grain (malt, flour) Medium / High Much aldehydes and complex esters. Requires careful organoleptic control. Collect 8–10% of AA.
Fruit (apples, pears, plums) Very high Maximum methanol risk due to pectins. Mandatory pectinase treatment of mash before fermentation! Collect 12–15% of AA.
Grape (pomace, chacha) High Skin and seeds contain much methanol and esters. Collect 10–12% of AA.
Potato Critically high Record holder for methanol. Requires double fractional distillation with increased head collection (15% of AA).

Chapter 8. Hardware: Help in Separating Heads

The design of your still directly affects how easily you can separate the poisons.

  • Thump keg (Thumper): Works excellently as a barrier for heads. Heavy ethers and aldehydes partially condense in it and don't reach the condenser. Tip: After collecting heads, be sure to drain the liquid from the thump keg, otherwise when heated it will evaporate and return to the "body."
  • Rectification column / Packed column: Provides multiple heat-mass exchange. Heads concentrate at the very top of the column. Before collecting the body, the column needs to be "stabilized" (run on itself for 15–20 minutes) so that poisons accumulate in the dephlegmator.
  • Copper elements: Copper catalytically binds sulfur compounds (which often come together with heads in grain mashes), converting them into insoluble copper sulfide (black coating). This doesn't remove methanol, but greatly purifies the aroma.

Chapter 9. First Distillation of Sugar Mash

The first distillation of sugar mash is the stage where 10–14% ABV liquid turns into 25–40% ABV raw spirit. At this stage, the final product is not created. Raw spirit is a semi-finished product, a concentrate that will be purified and separated into fractions during the second, fractional distillation.

Key specifics of sugar mash:

  • No aromatic value to preserve — the goal is maximum ethanol extraction.
  • Heads collection at first distillation is optional (many professionals skip it).
  • Fast distillation at maximum power is acceptable.
  • Collect all distillate into one container until strength in the stream drops to 20–25% ABV.

Chapter 10. First Distillation of Grain Mash

Grain mash requires a more careful approach than sugar mash:

  • Grain contains much aldehydes and complex esters — head collection is more important.
  • Collect the first 50–100 ml ("pre-head") to remove aggressive acetone fraction.
  • Sulfur compounds are common — copper elements in the still are recommended.
  • At second distillation, collect 8–10% of AA as heads.
  • The goal is to preserve the bread, malt character while removing poisons.

Chapter 11. First Distillation of Fruit & Berry Mash

Fruit and berry mashes are the most demanding in terms of head separation:

  • Maximum methanol risk due to pectins — mandatory pectinase treatment before fermentation.
  • At first distillation, collect the first 50–100 ml to remove aggressive fractions.
  • At second distillation, collect 10–15% of AA as heads (more than for sugar or grain).
  • The goal is to preserve delicate fruit aromas while ensuring safety.
  • Slow collection speed is critical — fast distillation destroys aromatic compounds.

Chapter 12. Typical Mistakes

Mistake Consequence Solution

Fast collection of heads Heavy impurities fly into the body Collect drop by drop (1–3 drops/sec)

Chapter 13. First Aid for Methanol Poisoning

🚑 Emergency: Methanol Poisoning

Methanol poisoning is a medical emergency. Every minute counts.

  1. Call emergency services immediately (112 in EU, 911 in US). Tell them you suspect methanol poisoning.
  2. Administer ethanol as antidote (if conscious): 100 ml of 40% vodka or 50 ml of 96% ethanol diluted in water. Ethanol competes with methanol for the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase, slowing methanol's conversion to toxic formaldehyde.
  3. Do NOT induce vomiting if the person is unconscious or having seizures.
  4. Keep the person warm and monitor breathing.
  5. At the hospital: Treatment includes fomepizole (4-methylpyrazole) — a specific antidote that blocks alcohol dehydrogenase, hemodialysis to remove methanol and formic acid, and supportive care.

Symptoms of methanol poisoning (appear 12–24 hours after ingestion): severe headache, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, visual disturbances (blurred vision, "snowstorm" before eyes), dilated pupils, difficulty breathing. In severe cases: blindness, coma, death.

Chapter 14. Legal Context

Before starting distillation, make sure you are acting within the framework of your country's legislation. In the EU, there is no single law on home distillation:

  • In Italy, Spain, Portugal, home distillation for personal consumption in limited volumes is allowed provided the tax authorities are notified.
  • In Germany, Austria, France, strict restrictions apply: registration, excise duty payment, and distillation volumes are limited.
  • In Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, there are historical traditions of home distilling (palinka, țuică, rakia), and legislation provides certain quotas for personal consumption.
  • In Scandinavian countries, home distillation is generally prohibited or strictly limited.

Methanol content in distillates is regulated by food safety standards in all EU countries. Proper head separation is not just a quality issue — it's a legal requirement for food safety.

Conclusion: The Price of Impatience

Separating head fractions is the foundation of drinking culture and distiller safety. This is the stage where a craftsman differs from a surrogate maker.

Losing 200–300 milliliters of raw spirit (equivalent to a bottle of vodka) from a 20-liter batch of mash is a negligible price for the guarantee that your product will bring joy, not resuscitation.

Three Golden Rules for Working with Heads

1. Calculate AA. Math gives the base from which you push off. Never collect heads "by eye" or "by time." Calculate absolute alcohol and collect 5–15% depending on raw material.
2. Drip, don't pour. Head collection speed is 1–2 drops per second. Haste here costs health. Slow collection ensures maximum vapor-phlegm contact and clear fraction separation.
3. Trust your nose, not the thermometer. Rub the distillate between your palms. If it smells of solvent — collection is not finished, even if the thermometer shows the "correct" temperature. Your nose is the final arbiter.

Respect the chemistry of the process, and it will give you drinks of the highest quality.

Introduction: The Magic of Reduced Pressure

In classical rectification at atmospheric pressure, ethanol boils at 78.37 °C and water at 100 °C. This is a temperature barrier through which every molecule present in the wash is forced to pass. For most tasks — producing neutral spirit, vodka, infused spirits — these temperatures are sufficient. But for creating delicate fruit, floral, and berry distillates, where the main value is fragile aromatic compounds, atmospheric rectification is a crude, destructive tool.

Lavender terpenes, rose essential oils, peach lactones, bergamot citrus aldehydes — all these molecules at 78 °C and above partially decompose, isomerize, oxidize, or produce undesirable thermal degradation products. The distillate loses freshness and acquires a "boiled," "jammy" character instead of the aroma of a fresh fruit or flower.

Vacuum rectification is a solution known to the perfume and pharmaceutical industries for over a hundred years. By reducing the pressure in the system, we lower the boiling point of all components in the mixture. Ethanol, which at atmospheric pressure boils at 78 °C, boils at just 34 °C at a pressure of 200 mbar. And at 50 mbar — just 12 °C. This opens the possibility of carrying out separation at temperatures comfortable for the most sensitive molecules.

In recent years, vacuum rectification units have appeared in the arsenal of craft distilleries creating premium eaux-de-vie, gins with botanical freshness, and aromatic concentrates for perfumery. This article is a detailed breakdown of the physics of the process, the equipment, and the practice of vacuum rectification.

Chapter 1. Physics of Vacuum Rectification: Why Pressure Changes Everything

1.1. The Clapeyron–Clausius Equation

The boiling point of a liquid is the temperature at which its saturated vapor pressure equals the external pressure. At atmospheric pressure of 1013 mbar (760 mmHg), ethanol boils at 78.37 °C. But if the external pressure is reduced, ethanol molecules need less energy to leave the liquid phase.

The dependence of boiling point on pressure is described by the Clapeyron–Clausius equation:

ln(P₂/P₁) = (ΔH/R) × (1/T₁ – 1/T₂)
where: P₁, P₂ — pressures; T₁, T₂ — boiling points at the corresponding pressures (in Kelvin); ΔH — specific heat of vaporization; R — universal gas constant (8.314 J/(mol·K)).
For ethanol, ΔH = 38.56 kJ/mol. Substituting known values, you can calculate the boiling point at any pressure.

1.3. Relative Volatility and Separation

Relative volatility (α) is the ratio of the concentrations of two components in the vapor phase to their ratio in the liquid phase. The higher α is, the easier it is to separate the components.

As pressure decreases, the relative volatility of most pairs increases. This means vacuum improves fraction separation, not just lowers temperature. For the ethanol–water mixture at 100 mbar, α increases by 5–10 % compared to atmospheric pressure.

Why this happens: at low pressure, the difference in boiling points of the components (in absolute degrees) becomes more significant relative to the absolute temperature. Ethanol and water at 30 °C "feel" the difference in volatility more strongly than at 78 °C.

1.4. The Azeotrope Under Vacuum

The ethanol–water azeotrope (95.6 % by volume) exists at atmospheric pressure. But at pressures below 70 mbar, the azeotrope disappears. This means that in a deep vacuum it is theoretically possible to obtain 100 % ethanol by simple rectification, without using molecular sieves or azeotropic additives.

In practice this is rarely used, because maintaining a pressure below 70 mbar requires a high-performance vacuum pump and flawless leak-tightness.

Chapter 2. What Vacuum Rectification Provides: Preserving Aroma

During classical distillation (78 °C and above), these molecules partially decompose or isomerize, giving the distillate a "boiled," "jammy," "turpentine" character.

2.2. The Effect of Vacuum on the Aromatic Profile

During rectification under a vacuum of 150–250 mbar (ethanol boiling point 34–41 °C):

  • Preservation of "top notes." The volatile terpenes and esters that during atmospheric distillation fly off with the heads or are destroyed remain in the body of the distillate.
  • Absence of thermal degradation. Delicate lactones and β-damascenone retain their original structure.
  • Reduced formation of furfural. Furfural (the smell of burnt bread, caramel) forms when sugars are heated above 100 °C. At 40 °C its formation is minimal.
  • Minimization of the Maillard reaction. In grain and honey washes at low temperatures, browning and the formation of heavy aromas do not occur.

Result: the distillate smells of fresh fruit or flower, not of jam or compote. This is a fundamental difference that distinguishes premium vacuum eaux-de-vie from traditional ones.

Chapter 3. Equipment for Vacuum Rectification

3.1. Main Components of the System

A vacuum rectification unit consists of:

  • Distillation pot — a leak-tight vessel able to withstand external pressure (internal rarefaction).
  • Rectification column — with packing, operating under vacuum conditions.
  • Dephlegmator — condenses part of the vapor and forms the reflux.
  • Condenser — condenses the product into the receiving vessel.
  • Vacuum pump — creates the rarefaction in the system.
  • Vacuum gauge — measures the pressure.
  • Pressure regulator — maintains the set rarefaction.
  • Safety valves — protection against excess pressure and vacuum.

3.2. Leak-Tightness Requirements

In a vacuum system, every connection must be absolutely leak-tight. An air leak into the system:

  • Raises the pressure, increasing the boiling point.
  • Introduces oxygen, which oxidizes aromatic compounds.
  • Can create a flammable mixture of alcohol vapors with air.
Connection Type Leak-Tightness Applicability

Soldering (copper, silver)Very highPermanent connections

Leak-tightness check: create a vacuum in the system, disconnect the pump, and observe the pressure for 10–15 minutes. If the pressure rises by more than 5 mbar, there is a leak that must be found and eliminated.

3.4. The Cold Trap

A cold trap is a device between the column and the vacuum pump, cooled to a low temperature (–20 °C and below). Purpose: to condense alcohol and aromatic compound vapors, preventing them from entering the pump.

Design: a U-shaped or spiral tube immersed in a cooling mixture (dry ice + acetone, –78 °C) or connected to a chiller.

Why it is needed:

  • Protects the oil pump from contamination.
  • Prevents loss of aromatic compounds.
  • Reduces the load on the pump.

3.5. Pressure Regulation

To maintain stable pressure, the following are used:

  • Manual needle valve — lets a small amount of air (or inert gas) into the system to raise the pressure. Simple and cheap, but requires constant operator attention.
  • Automatic pressure regulator — an electronic controller with a pressure sensor and a solenoid valve. Maintains the set pressure with an accuracy of ±1–2 mbar. Cost: €200–800.

Recommendation: for stable operation, an automatic regulator is preferable, especially during long distillations.

Chapter 4. Practical Setup of Vacuum Rectification

A

System Preparation

  1. Assembly and leak-tightness check. Assemble the system: pot → column → dephlegmator → condenser → cold trap → vacuum pump. Close all valves and taps. Turn on the pump and bring the pressure down to the operating value (e.g., 200 mbar). Disconnect the pump and close the valve in front of it. Observe the pressure for 15 minutes. A rise of no more than 5 mbar means the system is leak-tight.
  2. Loading the raw material. Pour in the low wines, diluted to 20–30 % by volume. Fill the pot to no more than 60 % of its volume (under vacuum the liquid can foam more strongly). Close the pot leak-tight.
  3. Connecting cooling. Supply water to the dephlegmator and condenser. Make sure the cold trap is cooled to –20 °C and below.
B

Startup and Stabilization

  1. Creating the vacuum. Turn on the vacuum pump. Smoothly reduce the pressure to the target value (e.g., 200 mbar). Turn on the automatic regulator to maintain the pressure.
  2. Heating the pot. Turn on the heating to minimum power. At a pressure of 200 mbar, the ethanol in the pot (20–30 % by volume) will start to boil at 40–45 °C. Wait until the vapor reaches the dephlegmator (indicated by the temperature at the top of the column).
  3. Stabilizing the column. Set full reflux (take-off tap closed). Let the column stabilize for 30–60 minutes. The temperature at the top of the column should settle at 34–35 °C (at 200 mbar this corresponds to the ethanol boiling point).
C

Fraction Take-Off

  1. Heads take-off. Calculate the heads volume (5–8 % of the absolute alcohol). Slightly open the take-off tap at a rate of 1–2 drops/second. Heads during vacuum rectification have a less pronounced solvent smell, but are determined organoleptically.
  2. Body take-off. Change the receiving vessel. Take off the body at a rate of 2–4 drops/second. Monitor the temperature at the top of the column: stable 34–35 °C at 200 mbar. Distillate strength: 93–96 % by volume (the same as with atmospheric rectification).
  3. Completing take-off. When the temperature at the top of the column rises above 36–37 °C, the body has ended. Stop take-off or switch to the tails vessel.
D

Completing the Process

  1. Shutdown. Turn off the pot heating. Let the system cool for 10–15 minutes with cooling still running. Smoothly let air into the system through the needle valve. Never open the system under vacuum — this can lead to splashing of hot liquid and damage to equipment. Turn off the vacuum pump. Disconnect the water supply to the dephlegmator and condenser. Disassemble the system and collect the product.

Chapter 5. Calculating Vacuum Rectification Parameters

5.1. Choosing the Operating Pressure

The operating pressure is chosen based on:

  • Sensitivity of the raw material. For the most delicate aromas (berries, flowers) — 50–150 mbar. For moderately sensitive (fruits, citrus) — 150–300 mbar.
  • Equipment capacity. The lower the pressure, the greater the pump capacity needed to maintain the vacuum at the given heating power.
  • Boiling point. At 200 mbar, ethanol boils at 40 °C — a comfortable temperature for most aromatic compounds.

5.2. Calculating the Boiling Point

For a quick estimate, use the simplified formula or table. Exact calculation:

T₂ = 1 / (1/T₁ – (R/ΔH) × ln(P₂/P₁))
where T is in Kelvin, P in mbar. For ethanol: T₁ = 351.52 K (78.37 °C), P₁ = 1013 mbar; ΔH = 38560 J/mol, R = 8.314 J/(mol·K).
Example: P₂ = 200 mbar → T₂ = 1/(1/351.52 – (8.314/38560) × ln(200/1013)) = 313 K = 39.9 °C.

5.3. Vacuum Pump Capacity

The pump capacity must exceed the amount of vapor formed in the pot; otherwise the pump cannot maintain the set pressure.

V_pump ≥ Q_heat / (ΔH × ρ_vapor) × (P_atm / P_operating)
where: Q_heat — heating power (W); ΔH — heat of vaporization (J/kg); ρ_vapor — vapor density at operating pressure (kg/m³); P_atm, P_operating — pressures (mbar).
Example: heating 1 kW (1000 W), pressure 200 mbar, ethanol. Vapor volume at 1013 mbar and 78 °C: 1000/846000 × (1/2.07) = 0.00057 m³/s = 2.05 m³/h. At 200 mbar the volume increases by 1013/200 = 5 times → 10.25 m³/h.
Conclusion: for a heating power of 1 kW at 200 mbar, you need a pump with a capacity of at least 12–15 m³/h.

Chapter 6. Applications: What Vacuum Rectification Is For

🫐 Delicate Berry Distillates

Raspberry, strawberry, blackberry, blueberry: aromatic compounds (raspberry ketone, ionones, lactones) are extremely sensitive to heat. Vacuum rectification at 100–150 mbar preserves the fresh, "living" berry aroma, unattainable with atmospheric distillation.

Premium eaux-de-vie, craft gins, perfume concentrates

🌸 Flower Distillates

Rose, jasmine, lavender, neroli: terpenes (linalool, geraniol, nerol) partially decompose at 78 °C. Vacuum rectification at 50–150 mbar allows obtaining distillates with the aroma of a fresh flower, not an "apothecary" tone.

Perfumery, premium gins, aromatic waters

🍋 Citrus Distillates

Bergamot, lime, yuzu, grapefruit: citrus aldehydes and terpenes (limonene, γ-terpinene) isomerize on heating, producing turpentine notes. Vacuum at 100–200 mbar preserves the fresh, green citrus profile.

New-style gins, aperitifs, cocktail ingredients

🌿 Botanical Gins

Modern craft gin distilleries use vacuum rectification to extract aromas from delicate botanicals: flowers, herbs, fresh leaves. This allows creating multi-layered, "fresh" gins in which each botanical ingredient is distinguishable.

Examples: The Botanist (Scotland), Monkey 47 (Germany)

🧴 Perfume Absolutes

In the perfume industry, vacuum distillation is used to obtain absolutes — highly concentrated aromatic extracts from flowers, resins, and spices. During atmospheric distillation many molecules are destroyed; during vacuum distillation they are preserved in their original form.

High-end perfumery

⚡ Energy Savings

During vacuum rectification, the pot is heated to 40–50 °C instead of 78–100 °C. This reduces energy consumption by 30–50 %. However, the savings are partially offset by the costs of running the vacuum pump and cooling the trap.

Chapter 7. Safety: Specific Risks of Vacuum Rectification

💥 Implosion

A system under vacuum is subjected to external atmospheric pressure (1013 mbar). The pot, column, and glass elements must withstand this pressure without deformation.

Precautions: use equipment designed for operation under vacuum (vacuum flasks, thick-walled glass). Do not use thin-walled glass vessels. Protect glass elements with casings or nets. Wear safety glasses when working with vacuum.

🔥 Vapor Explosion

Alcohol vapors mixed with air are explosive at concentrations of 3.5–15 % by volume. In a vacuum system the oxygen concentration is reduced, but if air leaks in, a flammable mixture can form.

Precautions: maintain leak-tightness. Use inert gas (nitrogen) to equalize pressure instead of air. Ensure ventilation of the room. Do not use open flames.

🌬️ Letting Air In Under Vacuum

When completing the process, never open the system abruptly. Rapid air intake can splash hot liquid, create a flammable mixture of vapors with oxygen, and damage fragile elements (glass, gaskets).

Correct procedure: smoothly let air in through the needle valve over 2–5 minutes, until the pressure equalizes with atmospheric.

↩️ Liquid Backflow

When the vacuum pump is abruptly disconnected, liquid from the receiving vessel or cold trap can be sucked back into the system. This contaminates the product and can damage the pump.

Precautions: install a check valve between the system and the pump. Before disconnecting the pump, smoothly let air in. Use a liquid trap in front of the pump.

❄️ Moisture Freezing

At low pressures (50–100 mbar) and boiling points of 13–26 °C, water vapor can condense in the cold trap and freeze on the walls, reducing throughput.

Precaution: regularly defrost the trap.

Chapter 10. Frequently Asked Questions

Q Can I use an ordinary moonshine still for vacuum rectification?

Only if it is designed for operation under vacuum (leak-tight flanged connections, thick-walled elements). Most household stills with clamps and threaded connections will not provide sufficient leak-tightness. Modernization is possible, but requires replacing seals and installing vacuum fittings.

Q Can I obtain 100 % ethanol under vacuum?

Theoretically — yes. At pressures below 70 mbar the ethanol–water azeotrope disappears, and the separation becomes complete. But in practice, maintaining such a deep vacuum for several hours requires a high-performance pump and ideal leak-tightness. For most tasks, 95–96 % is sufficient.

Q Is vacuum rectification slower than atmospheric?

Usually yes. At a lower boiling point, the heating power must be lower to avoid foaming. This increases distillation time by 30–50 %. However, the product quality is significantly higher.

Q Can vacuum rectification be carried out without a dephlegmator?

No. A dephlegmator is necessary to form the reflux and ensure heat and mass transfer. Without it, the system operates like simple distillation, even under vacuum.

Q How difficult is it to maintain the vacuum for 6–8 hours?

With the correct setup of an automatic regulator — not difficult. The system automatically maintains the set pressure, compensating for leaks and fluctuations. The operator's main task is to monitor the temperature and fraction take-off.

Q Can vacuum be used for fractional distillation of fruit wash (without rectification)?

Yes. Vacuum fractional distillation (without a column, with a simple pot and dephlegmator) is an excellent method for obtaining aromatic eaux-de-vie. The temperature in the pot of 40–50 °C instead of 78–90 °C significantly reduces thermal impact on aromatic compounds.

Q Which vacuum pump should I choose for a home unit?

A membrane (oil-free) pump with a capacity of 10–15 m³/h and a maximum rarefaction of 20–50 mbar. Cost €800–1,500. Avoid cheap oil pumps: alcohol vapors quickly contaminate the oil and put the pump out of action.

Q Is a cold trap needed?

Strongly recommended. Without it, alcohol vapors enter the pump, reducing its performance and service life. The simplest trap is a U-shaped tube in dry ice with acetone (–78 °C) or in a freezer (–20 °C).

Q Can vacuum rectification be used for sugar wash?

Technically possible, but not expedient. Sugar wash contains no delicate aromas that need to be preserved. The additional costs of vacuum equipment do not pay off through improved quality.

Q At what pressure is it best to work?

For most tasks — 150–250 mbar. This gives an ethanol boiling point of 34–41 °C, sufficient to preserve most aromatic compounds. A deeper vacuum (50–100 mbar) is needed only for extremely sensitive flowers and berries.

Q Is vacuum rectification safer than atmospheric?

Not necessarily. The risks are different: implosion instead of vapor ejection, the need for leak-tightness, the danger of rapid air intake. Each system requires its own set of precautions.

11.2. When It Is Justified

Vacuum rectification is economically justified if:

  • You produce premium distillates with high added value (craft gins, eaux-de-vie, perfume concentrates).
  • The aroma of the raw material is the main value, and its loss during atmospheric distillation is critical.
  • Production volumes are sufficient to pay off the equipment (from 500 liters of distillate per year and more).
  • You work with extremely delicate botanicals (flowers, fresh leaves, rare berries).

For home moonshining aimed at obtaining neutral spirit, infused spirits, and basic distillates, vacuum rectification is excessive.

Conclusion: Vacuum as a Tool for Delicate Tasks

Vacuum rectification is not a replacement for atmospheric rectification, but its complement for specific tasks. It solves one concrete problem: preserving fragile aromatic molecules that are destroyed at 78 °C. Lavender terpenes, peach lactones, bergamot citrus aldehydes, raspberry ketone — all of them preserve their structure and aroma only during low-temperature separation.

Three Rules to Remember

Vacuum lowers the boiling point, not the strength. At 200 mbar, ethanol boils at 41 °C, but the distillate strength remains 93–96 % by volume. The physics of separation does not change — only the temperature regime changes.
Leak-tightness is an absolute requirement. An air leak raises the pressure, increases the boiling point, and destroys the aroma. Every connection must be vacuum-tight. A leak-tightness check before every distillation is mandatory.
Vacuum is a tool for premium products. For neutral spirit and basic infused spirits, atmospheric rectification is sufficient and significantly cheaper. Vacuum is justified where the aroma of the raw material is the main value and its loss is unacceptable: craft gins, flower eaux-de-vie, perfume absolutes.

Understanding the physics of vacuum rectification opens a new level of control for the distiller. This is a transition from "extracting alcohol" to "extracting aroma in its pristine form." Where atmospheric distillation gives a "boiled compote," vacuum distillation gives a "fresh fruit just picked from the branch." The difference is 30 degrees Celsius separating 78 °C from 48 °C, and the molecules that these 30 degrees destroy or preserve.

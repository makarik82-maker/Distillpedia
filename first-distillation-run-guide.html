Introduction: The Moment of Truth for the Distiller

Fermentation is complete. The hydrometer reads 0–2 °P, the taste is dry, the airlock is silent. It seems you can pour the mash into the still immediately and start the first run. But experienced distillers know: between "the mash is ready" and "the mash is optimal for the first run" there is a gap of 24–72 hours, and this gap determines the quality of the low wines, the cleanliness of the equipment after distillation, and even the safety of the process.

The first run is not simply about obtaining an alcohol-containing liquid. It is about extracting the maximum amount of ethanol from the mash while transferring the minimum amount of by-products into the distillate. And the success of this operation begins not with turning on the still's heating, but with correctly choosing the moment when to stop fermentation and prepare the mash for distillation.

In this article we will examine why rushing the first run is costly, how to determine the optimal time window, and which preparatory steps turn a merely ready mash into the ideal raw material for distillation.

Chapter 1. What Is the First Run and Its Goals

1.1. Definition and Place in the Process

The first run (also called "stripping run" or "low wines run") is the distillation of the mash without fraction cuts. The entire distillate from start to finish is collected into a single container. The goal is to extract the maximum ethanol from the mash, producing low wines at 25–40% ABV.

1.2. Goals of the First Run

✅ What the First Run Achieves

  • Maximum ethanol yield. Extract all the alcohol the yeast produced.
  • Concentration. Raise the strength from 8–18% ABV in the mash to 25–40% ABV in the low wines, reducing volume 3–5 fold.
  • Partial purification. Separate ethanol from non-volatile impurities (yeast, proteins, minerals), which remain in the boiler.
  • Preparation for the second run. Create a raw material with predictable strength and composition for precise fraction cuts.

❌ What Is NOT Done During the First Run

  • No fraction cuts. Heads and tails are not separated — that is the task of the second run.
  • No stream-strength control. Collection continues until the alcohol runs out (usually down to 5–10% ABV in the boiler, or 98–99 °C).
  • No final purification. Low wines are an intermediate product that require a second run.

Chapter 2. Why the Timing of the First Run Is Critical

🔥

2.1. Scorching of Sugar and Yeast

What happens: Suspended yeast cells settle on the boiler bottom and heating element. Above 100 °C the yeast proteins denature and scorch. Residual sugar caramelizes even faster (160–180 °C).

Consequences:

  • Burnt taste — pyrolysis products (furfural, HMF, pyrazines) transfer into the low wines.
  • Reduced heating efficiency — the scorched layer acts as insulation.
  • Difficult equipment cleaning.
  • Risk of local overheating and heater failure.
Solution: clarify the mash and decant it off the sediment before the run. Clarification takes time — those very 24–72 hours.
🫧

2.2. Foaming and Mash Eruption

What happens: During active fermentation the mash is saturated with dissolved CO₂. On heating, gas solubility drops and CO₂ escapes, creating abundant foam that can climb into the condenser and collection vessel.

Consequences:

  • Contaminated distillate (yeast, proteins, raw-material particles).
  • Clogged equipment (condenser, dephlegmator, column).
  • Product loss.
  • Danger: hot mash can splash out, creating a burn hazard.
Solution: let the mash "rest" 24–72 hours after fermentation ends. Excess CO₂ escapes and foaming on heating becomes minimal.
📉

2.3. Yield Loss from Incomplete Fermentation

What happens: if you start the first run while fermentation is not yet complete (gravity above 3–4 °P), some sugar remains unfermented.

Consequences:

  • Direct ethanol loss. Each percent of residual sugar is potential alcohol not produced. At 5 °P in 20 L, roughly 100 g of sugar remains — about 50 ml of lost absolute alcohol.
  • Caramelization in the boiler. Unfermented sugar scorches, giving a burnt taste.
  • Risk of secondary fermentation. If low wines are stored, residual sugar can ferment in a sealed container, building pressure.
Solution: confirm fermentation is fully complete (gravity 0–2 °P) before the first run.
🦠

2.4. Infection from Over-Standing

What happens: if the mash stands too long after fermentation (more than 7–10 days at room temperature), foreign microorganisms develop:

  • Acetic acid bacteria oxidize ethanol into acetic acid.
  • Lactic acid bacteria produce lactic and butyric acids.
  • Molds grow on the surface, producing mycotoxins.

Consequences: reduced alcohol yield, sour unpleasant smell, toxicity (mycotoxins are heat-resistant). Severely infected mash cannot be saved — pour it out.

Solution: do not delay the first run. Optimal window — 24–72 hours. If storage is needed, cool the mash to 5–10 °C.

3.2. How to Confirm Fermentation Has Ended

This was covered in detail in the previous article, but here is a quick reminder of the criteria:

  • Gravity: 0–2 °P (0.998–1.008 SG) by hydrometer, stable for 24–48 hours.
  • Taste: dry, slightly bitter, without sweetness.
  • Appearance: foam has dropped, the mash is clarifying (or stays cloudy due to the raw material), yeast is settling.
  • Airlock: silent (but this is not the main criterion).
⚠ Important: measure gravity twice, 24 hours apart. If both readings are the same, fermentation is truly complete.

Chapter 4. Preparing the Mash for the First Run

4.1. Clarification and Decanting

Primary

Goal: separate the clarified mash from the yeast sediment to minimize scorching in the boiler.

Procedure:

  1. Confirm fermentation is complete and the mash has rested 24–72 hours.
  2. Prepare a clean vessel for decanting (a second fermenter or the still itself, if removable).
  3. Carefully siphon or tilt-pour the clarified mash off the sediment.
  4. Do not disturb the sediment. Better to leave 0.5–1 L with the sediment than to pour in turbidity.

The residue with sediment can be: poured out (small volume), distilled separately (lower quality), or added to the next sugar wash batch to reuse yeast (sugar wash only).

Result: clarified mash with minimal suspended yeast. Scorching in the boiler is reduced 3–5 fold.

4.2. Cold Crash (Optional)

Optional

For maximum clarification:

  1. After fermentation ends, move the vessel to a cool place (5–10 °C) for 24–48 hours.
  2. At low temperature, yeast and suspended particles settle faster and denser.
  3. Decant the clarified mash.

Especially useful for: fruit and berry mashes with pectins, grain mashes with proteins, and hard-water mashes with calcium salts.

4.3. Bentonite Treatment (Optional)

Optional

Bentonite is a natural clay that adsorbs proteins and suspended particles, accelerating clarification.

Procedure:

  1. Dissolve 1–2 g of bentonite per 10 L of mash in a small amount of warm water (200–300 ml).
  2. Stir thoroughly to a uniform suspension.
  3. Pour into the mash and stir.
  4. Leave for 12–24 hours.
  5. Decant the clarified mash.

When to use: the mash remains cloudy after 72 hours; you plan rectification and want the cleanest low wines; grain or potato mash with high protein content.

When NOT to use: fruit mashes for aromatic distillates (bentonite can adsorb some aromatic compounds); if you are in a hurry (bentonite takes 12–24 hours).

4.4. Degassing (CO₂ Removal)

Helpful

Even after 24–72 hours the mash contains dissolved CO₂. On heating it escapes and creates foam.

Methods:

  • Natural: simply wait 24–72 hours. CO₂ escapes on its own.
  • Mechanical: stir the mash with a drill fitted with a food-grade mixer attachment for 2–3 minutes. Speeds up CO₂ release. Caution: foam may form — do not fill the vessel more than 70%.
  • Vacuum: apply vacuum above the mash with a pump (rarely used at home).

Recommendation: for most cases, natural degassing over 24–72 hours is sufficient.

Chapter 5. Specifics for Different Mash Types

🍬

5.1. Sugar Mash

  • Clarifies quickly (24–48 hours is enough).
  • Yeast sediment is dense, easily decanted.
  • Low protein and pectin content — minimal scorching.

Optimal timing: 24–48 hours. Preparation: decanting. Bentonite not needed.

🌾

5.2. Grain Mash (wheat, rye, corn)

  • Stays cloudy even after clarification (proteins, grain particles).
  • Yeast sediment is loose, mixed with spent grains.
  • High protein content — scorching risk.

Optimal timing: 48–72 hours. Preparation: decanting; bentonite desirable; cold crash effective.

Tip: if the mash is very thick, dilute with water (10–20% of volume) before the run to reduce viscosity.

🍎

5.3. Fruit Mash (apples, grapes, pears)

  • Clarifies slowly (48–72 hours minimum).
  • Contains pectins that do not fully settle.
  • Yeast sediment is mixed with pulp particles.

Optimal timing: 48–72 hours. Preparation: decanting; bentonite NOT recommended (adsorbs aroma); cold crash effective.

🍒

5.4. Berry Mash (cherry, plum, raspberry)

  • Very slow clarification (72 hours or more).
  • High pectin content.
  • Small berry particles do not settle.

Optimal timing: 72 hours. Preparation: decanting; bentonite NOT recommended; cold crash is essential.

Tip: do not aim for full transparency. Berry mash will remain slightly cloudy — this is normal.

🥔

5.5. Potato Mash

  • Very thick and cloudy.
  • Never clarifies completely (pectins, proteins, starch).
  • High scorching risk.

Optimal timing: 48–72 hours. Preparation: decanting (as much as possible); bentonite desirable; dilute with water (20–30%) to reduce viscosity.

Tip: potato mash is best distilled in stills with a steam jacket or steam generator to avoid direct contact with the heater.

Chapter 6. Storing Ready Mash Before the Run

6.1. When Storage Is Needed

Sometimes it is impossible to distill within the optimal window (24–72 hours). Reasons: lack of time, needing to accumulate several batches for one large run, or waiting for available equipment.

6.2. Storage Conditions

Temperature Max Duration Risk How to Ensure Requirements
Room (18–22 °C) 3–5 days Yeast autolysis, bacterial infection Normal indoor conditions Airlock in place, minimal air contact
Cold (5–10 °C) 1–2 weeks Minimal — cold slows autolysis and bacteria Refrigerator, cellar, cold room Sealed container (airlock may not work without gas emission)
Frozen (−18 °C and below) Up to 3 months Minimal — metabolism fully stopped Freezer Stir before distilling (water crystallizes, alcohol concentrates)

6.3. Signs of Spoilage During Storage

If any of these appear during storage, it is better to pour the mash out:

  • Mold on the surface (white, gray, green, black).
  • Sharp sour smell (vinegar).
  • Smell of rotten eggs, vomit, or mold.
  • Slippery film on the surface.
Light souring (mild acidity, no mold) is not a catastrophe. Distill immediately, with an increased heads cut during the second run.

Chapter 7. Technical Aspects of the First Run

7.1. Filling the Boiler

Recommended volume: no more than 2/3 (65–70%) of the boiler's capacity.

  • On heating, the mash foams and expands.
  • Space is needed for steam formation.
  • Overfilling risks a mash eruption through the steam outlet.

Example: for a 30 L boiler, pour no more than 20 L of mash.

7.2. Heating Speed

The first run is fast. Unlike the second (fractional) run, slow, precise distillation is not needed here. The goal is to extract the maximum alcohol in minimum time.

  • Heat at maximum power until collection begins.
  • After collection starts, slightly reduce power for stability.
  • Total time for 20 L of mash: 2–4 hours (depends on heating power).

7.3. Collecting the Low Wines

No fraction cuts. The entire distillate from start to finish is collected into one container.

When to stop collection — three criteria:

20–30% ABV
🔥

When the boiler temperature reaches

98–99 °C
📏

30–40% of mash volume
Recommendation: use a combination of criteria. Stop collection when the boiler temperature is 98–99 °C AND the stream strength is below 20–30% ABV.

7.4. What Remains in the Boiler

After the first run, spent wash (stillage) remains in the boiler, containing:

  • Water.
  • Non-volatile substances (yeast, proteins, minerals).
  • Residual sugar (if fermentation was incomplete).
  • Heavy fusel oils (partially).
  • Glycerol.

Strength of the spent wash: 0.5–3% ABV (residual alcohol that is not economical to extract).

Disposal: pour down the drain (small volumes); use as fertilizer (dilute 1:10 with water); add to compost.

7.5. Result of the First Run

Parameter Low Wines
Strength 25–40% ABV (depends on mash strength and stopping point)
Volume ~25–35% of the mash volume
Composition Ethanol + water + volatile impurities (heads, tails, some fusel oils)
Appearance Clear or slightly cloudy
Smell Alcoholic, with impurities (normal for low wines)

Next steps: dilute with water to 20–30% ABV (optimal for the second run), then perform the second run with fractional cuts.

Chapter 8. Typical Mistakes During the First Run

Mistake Consequence Solution
Distilling immediately after fermentation ends (no 24–72 h wait) Foaming, mash eruption, distillate contamination Wait 24–72 hours for degassing and clarification
Distilling without decanting (with sediment) Yeast scorching, burnt taste, difficult boiler cleaning Decant the mash off the sediment
Distilling an incomplete fermentation (gravity > 3–4 °P) Yield loss, sugar caramelization, burnt taste Confirm fermentation is complete (0–2 °P)
Overfilling the boiler (more than 70%) Mash eruption, equipment contamination Fill no more than 2/3 of the boiler
Stopping too early (temperature < 95 °C) Yield loss, alcohol remains in the spent wash Collect up to 98–99 °C in the boiler
Storing the mash more than 7 days at room temperature Infection, alcohol loss, sour smell Cool to 5–10 °C for long storage
Using cloudy mash for rectification Column clogging, scorching Clarify the mash before the run

Chapter 9. Practical Algorithm: From End of Fermentation to First Run

1

Confirm Fermentation Has Ended

  • Measure gravity: 0–2 °P. Repeat after 24 hours — stable.
  • Taste: dry, without sweetness.
  • If gravity is above 3 °P, fermentation is not complete. Take action (see the stalled-fermentation article).
2

Maturation Period (24–72 Hours)

  • Leave the mash in the fermenter at 18–22 °C with the airlock in place.
  • Do not open unnecessarily.
  • Optimal: 48 hours for sugar, 72 hours for grain and fruit mashes.
3

Clarification (Optional)

  • If the mash is cloudy, consider a cold crash (5–10 °C, 24 hours).
  • For grain and potato mashes — bentonite treatment (1–2 g per 10 L, 12–24 hours).
4

Decanting

  • Carefully siphon the clarified mash off the sediment.
  • Leave 0.5–1 L with the sediment.
5

Filling the Boiler

  • Pour the mash into the boiler — no more than 2/3 of the volume.
  • Confirm the boiler is clean and the seals are in good condition.
  • Connect the condenser and supply water.
6

The First Run

  • Heat at maximum power until collection begins.
  • Collect without separating fractions.
  • Stop when the boiler reaches 98–99 °C or stream strength drops below 20%.
7

Result

  • Collected low wines at 25–40% ABV.
  • Spent wash in the boiler (dispose of it).
  • Dilute the low wines to 20–30% ABV and prepare for the second run.

Chapter 10. Frequently Asked Questions

Q Can I distill the mash immediately after fermentation ends, without waiting 24–72 hours?
Technically yes, but it is not recommended. Risk of foaming, scorching, and distillate contamination. If time is critical, at least wait 12–24 hours and decant thoroughly.
Q The mash has been sitting for 2 weeks. Can I still distill it?
It depends on the storage conditions. If at 5–10 °C — yes, distill it. If at room temperature — evaluate the smell and appearance. If there are signs of infection (sour smell, mold), it is better to pour it out.
Q Do I need to dilute the mash before the first run?
Usually no. But if the mash is very thick (grain, potato) or the strength is above 18% ABV, you can dilute with water by 10–20% to reduce viscosity and scorching risk.
Q Can I mix several batches of mash before distillation?
Yes, if they are the same type and both are ready. Mixing increases volume and makes the run more efficient. Make sure all batches have fully fermented.
Q What should I do with the yeast sediment after decanting?
Options: pour it out (simplest); distill it separately in a small vessel (lower quality); add it to the next sugar wash as a "starter" (sugar wash only, not fruit or grain).
Q The low wines came out cloudy. Is this normal?
Yes, this is normal. Cloudiness is caused by volatile substances that precipitated on cooling or dilution. After the second run, the distillate will be clear.
Q Can I store low wines before the second run?
Yes, low wines are more stable than mash. Store them in a sealed container at room temperature for up to several months. Before the second run, dilute to 20–30% ABV.
Q The first run took 5 hours. Is this normal?
It depends on volume and power. For 20 L of mash on a 2 kW still, 3–4 hours is normal. 5 hours is a bit long but acceptable. If it takes longer, check the heating power and boiler insulation.

Conclusion: Patience Pays Off

The first run is not a sprint but a marathon with a proper start. The moment you decide to begin distillation determines not only the alcohol yield, but also the quality of the low wines, the cleanliness of the equipment, and your enjoyment of the process.

Three Rules to Remember

⏳ Don't Rush 24–72 hours after fermentation ends is not wasted time — it is an investment in quality. During this time, the mash clarifies, degasses, and stabilizes.
🍶 Decant Separating the mash from the yeast sediment is the most effective way to prevent scorching and burnt taste. 5 minutes of work saves hours of boiler cleaning.
🚫 Don't Delay The optimal readiness window is 24–72 hours. After that, infection and autolysis risk rise. If you cannot run on time — cool the mash.

The first run is the foundation for the second, fractional run. The cleaner and higher-quality the low wines, the easier the fraction cuts and the smoother the final distillate. Understanding the optimal moment for the first run turns a distiller from someone who "just runs the still" into a master managing the process.

Happy first run and clean low wines.

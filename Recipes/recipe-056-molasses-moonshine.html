🍯 Molasses Moonshine

Molasses moonshine largely resembles sugar moonshine but sometimes carries a specific smell and taste. It is worth making only when molasses came free — for example expired stock that needs processing. The second run is best done on a rectification column to obtain clean spirit.

Category
Sugar
ABV
40–45%
Difficulty
Medium
Fermentation
4–14 days
Yield
up to 780 ml/kg (40%)
⚠️ Educational purposes only. Home distillation is illegal in many countries without a licence. Check your local laws first.

Theory

Molasses is a by-product of sugar and starch production. Distillers like it for its low price and high sugar content — 60–80%. At an average 65% sugar content, 1 kg of molasses can give up to 780 ml of 40° spirit; in practice the yield is 15–20% lower.

However, the quality of molasses moonshine is about the same as sugar moonshine, or worse. There are many molasses types (malt, beet, corn, potato, other starch raw materials) and factories use different technologies, so it is hard to judge raw-material quality in advance. The exception is genuine cane molasses, from which you can make home rum close to the original.

💡 Use only molasses intended for confectionery (expired is fine). Other types may give an unpleasant aroma and taste. Ideally, make spirit rather than moonshine from this raw material: use a rectification column for the second run instead of a simple still.

Ingredients

  • Molasses — 10 l
  • Water — 30 l (adjustable: wash sugar must stay at 18–20%)
  • Yeast — 200 g pressed baker's, 40 g dry, or spirit yeast per 40 l of must

Wash Preparation

1
Bring half of the water to a boil, pour the boiling water into the molasses and stir until a homogeneous syrup forms. Molasses (especially crystallised) dissolves well only in hot water, and boiling kills pathogenic microorganisms that could infect the wash.
2
Pour the syrup into the fermentation vessel and add the second half of the water. Stir. Fill the vessel to max 75% of volume, leaving room for CO₂ and foam.
3
When the must cools below 30 °C, pitch the yeast and stir again. Never pitch yeast above 30 °C or they may die and fermentation will not start.
4
Fit any airlock and move the molasses wash to a dark place (or cover with dense fabric) at 20–28 °C. Leave until fermentation ends.
⚠️ Because of the specific smell during fermentation, do not keep the wash in a flat or house — move it to a utility room with good ventilation.
5
The wash is ready when the airlock stops releasing gas (glove deflates), the wash lightens, stops being sweet (a minimal sweetness from caramel that yeast cannot process is possible), and a sediment layer forms at the bottom.

Distillation

6
Rack the finished wash off the sediment so molasses residues do not scorch on heating and make the spirit bitter.
7
First run at maximum speed without fraction separation. Collect the first 150–200 ml of output separately and discard it — this fraction contains many harmful substances. End collection when the stream strength falls below 30%.
8
Measure the first-run spirit strength and compute the pure alcohol: volume in litres × strength % ÷ 100. Dilute with water to 18–20%.
9
Second run: collect the first 15% of the pure-alcohol amount separately as "heads" — a technical fraction that must not be drunk.
10
Collect the "body" until the stream strength falls below 45%. Then stop, or collect "tails" into a separate vessel.
11
Dilute the "body" with water to 40–45% ABV, bottle in glass, seal tightly and rest for 2–3 days in a cool place for taste stabilisation.
💡 For a clean neutral spirit, run the second distillation on a rectification column. If you use genuine cane molasses, follow a rum programme instead: pot-still both runs and age on charred oak.

🥝 Kiwi Moonshine

An original fruit-citrus aroma with lime tones; the taste shows notes of gooseberry, green apple, melon and tropical fruit. A rare distillate you cannot buy in a shop.

Category
Fruit
ABV
40–45%
Difficulty
Medium
Fermentation
5–45 days
Yield
96–144 ml/kg (40%)
⚠️ Educational purposes only. Home distillation is illegal in many countries without a licence. Check your local laws first.

Choosing the Ingredients

Kiwi

Any kiwi condition works: crushed, mis-stored, undersized, etc. — as long as there is no rot or mould (problem parts can be cut off). Home distillers usually arrange with market sellers to take unsold leftovers at a reasonable price. Green kiwi is more aromatic, but yellow pulp keeps more fruit tones. Very firm fruit should rest for 4–5 days at room temperature, otherwise a sour note appears in the aftertaste.

Yield

Kiwi contains 8–12% sugar (usually about 9%). Theoretically 1 kg gives 96–144 ml of 40° spirit; the practical yield is 15–20% lower because part of the ethanol is lost in two runs.

Sugar

1 kg of beet or cane sugar adds 1.2 L of 40% spirit but dulls the quality with molasses and kvass tones, so do not overdo it. Experimentally, up to 150 g of sugar per 1 kg of kiwi is acceptable (optimum 80–100 g). Fructose and dextrose have no organoleptics of their own and only "smear" the kiwi notes with ethanol sharpness; add them in the same proportion.

Water

Clean juice is almost impossible to press from kiwi without losing 50–60% of the sugar, and a thick must ferments poorly, sours easily and is hard to distil. Water must be non-chlorinated (chlorine suppresses yeast): well, column, street dispenser or filtered; tap water stood 3 days in an open vessel also works. Dilute the finished spirit only with softened water (bottled is best); boiled water lacks oxygen and microelements and is the worst choice for both wash and dilution.

Yeast or starter

There are no wild yeast on kiwi skin because tropical fruit is treated with preservatives for long storage. Best: cultured wine yeast (universal or for white grape juice), or a fresh-berry/raisin starter set 4–5 days ahead — cultured yeast activates faster, ferments more stably and produces less foam than a starter. Spirit yeast finishes in 4–8 days but the organoleptics are poorer. Fruit-wash yeast (with pectinase) is justified for unripe kiwi with much pectin (less methanol). Dry or pressed baker's yeast leaves almost no kiwi tones and adds a bread-kvass taste — use only when nothing else is at hand and the pulp must be processed quickly.

Ingredients

  • Kiwi — 5 kg (whole fruit weight)
  • Water — 10 l, plus 4 l per 1 kg of sugar
  • Sugar (optional) — up to 150 g per 1 kg of kiwi
  • Yeast — per wash volume (baker's: 100 g pressed or 20 g dry per 1 kg of total sugar)

Wash Preparation

1
Peel the kiwi — otherwise an unpleasant earthy taste appears in the spirit. Cut out blackened, mouldy and rotten parts.
2
Halve each fruit, place them in a plastic bag, seal tightly and leave for 24 hours in the freezer so the negative temperature breaks down non-fermentable polysaccharides into glucose and fructose, partially destroys acids and releases aromatic substances.
3
Thaw the kiwi at room temperature, then mash by hand to a puree. A blender or meat grinder damages the seeds and may add a slight bitterness.
4
Place the mass into the fermentation vessel. Dilute the yeast per the manufacturer's instructions, or use the universal activation method.
5
Dissolve the sugar in water in a separate vessel, pour the syrup (or only water) into the kiwi pulp and stir to a homogeneous consistency. If the must is thick (hard to stir by hand), add more water. Fill the vessel to max 75% of volume, leaving room for foam and CO₂ (kiwi wash foams heavily).
6
Pitch the yeast or starter, fit any airlock and keep the vessel in a dark place (or covered) at 18–28 °C. Fermentation must start within 36 hours: gas from the airlock (glove inflates) and foam. If not, find and fix the cause urgently or the must will sour.
7
For the first 3–4 days watch the wash so the floating pulp does not block the airlock outlet. Open and stir as needed, submerging the pulp in the liquid. After a few days almost all pulp settles and stops floating.
8
Fermentation lasts 5–45 days depending on yeast, sugar and temperature. It is finished when: gas release stops or the glove deflates; a loose sediment layer forms at the bottom; foam has disappeared from the surface (patches up to 3 cm may remain); the wort has partly lightened; the wash smells of alcohol and has no sweetness.
9
Rack the finished wash off the sediment (preferably through a thin tube); the pomace may be lightly pressed. Strain through a fine sieve or cheesecloth to remove pulp residues that could scorch and make the spirit bitter.

Distillation

10
Pour the wash into the still, filling 30–70% of volume. Assemble the equipment as a simple pot still: column up to 60 cm with no packing, all auxiliary units off, leaving only a coil or shell-and-tube condenser and one thump keg if the design has one. Factory columns: pot still mode.
11
First run at maximum cooler capacity: the distillate may run warm and cloudy but never steamy. Stop when the stream strength falls below 20%. Running almost "to zero" is acceptable if the extra energy, water and time seem justified by the small gain.
12
Measure the raw spirit strength at 20 °C (or correct in a calculator) and compute the pure alcohol: volume in litres × strength % ÷ 100. Dilute to 18–20%. Do not clean with any means (including activated carbon) — keep the delicate fruit organoleptics.
13
Second run: after boiling, reduce power so the distillate drips slowly and stays cold, almost like the inlet water. Collect the first 14% of the pure-alcohol amount separately as "heads" — a harmful technical fraction.
15
Raise power so the spirit drips fast or runs in a thin stream but stays cold. Collect the "body" until the stream strength falls below 42%, then stop or collect "tails" separately for future rectification.
16
Dilute the kiwi moonshine ("body") with softened water to 40–45%, bottle in glass, seal tightly and rest for at least 5 days in a cool place. Shelf life is unlimited.
💡 The 24-hour freeze step is what converts non-fermentable polysaccharides into fermentable sugars and releases the aroma — do not skip it for firm fruit.

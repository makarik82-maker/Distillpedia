🍐 Quince Moonshine (Balkan Dunevacha)

In Balkan countries quince moonshine is called "Dunevacha" (from the Serbian "dunja" — quince). It is one of the most aromatic fruit distillates — simultaneously with notes of apple, pear, lemon, flowers, vanilla and spices — while the taste of the drink is soft and fruity. Below is the correct quince wash recipe and the double distillation technology that preserves this unique organoleptic profile.

Category
Fruit
ABV
40–45%
Difficulty
Medium
Fermentation
5–45 days
Yield
~15 kg quince
⚠️ Legal & Safety Disclaimer: This recipe is provided for educational purposes only. Distilling alcohol at home is illegal in many countries without a proper licence. Always check your local laws. Read our Distillation Safety Guide before attempting any distillation.

Choosing the Ingredients

Quince

Any ripe quince is suitable — without green inclusions on the skin: ordinary (Muskatnaya, Anzherskaya, Zolotistaya, etc.) or Japanese. In the second case the aroma is better expressed, but the moonshine yield is lower and a barely perceptible astringency appears. Unripe fruit has an increased pectin concentration, from which yeast releases more methanol, intensifying the hangover. If quality fruit is plentiful, you can make quince wine first and distill it by this method if the drink does not appeal.

Yield: depending on ripeness and growing region, Japanese quince contains 6–8% sugar, ordinary quince 8–13%. This means 1 kg of fruit theoretically gives 72–156 ml of 40° moonshine, but in practice ethanol losses after two distillations reach 20%.

Sugar, Water, Yeast

  • Sugar (optional): 1 kg of cane or beet sugar increases the moonshine yield by 1.2 L (40%), but the drink worsens with kvass and molasses notes, so a compromise between quantity and quality is important. Empirically, up to 100 g of sugar per 1 kg of ordinary quince can be added, and up to 150 g for Japanese quince. Sugar substitutes — fructose and dextrose (powdered glucose) — affect organoleptics less and are added in the same proportion; they only "blur" the fruit profile with pure alcohol tones. Store sugar (dextrose, fructose) in a tightly closed container so crystals do not absorb foreign smells and mold spores.
  • Water: quince is not juicy enough (300–500 ml of juice per 1 kg), and thick wash ferments poorly and is hard to filter before distillation, so the wort is diluted with water. Water also controls total sugar content (must not exceed 20%) when artificial sugar is added. Any drinking water is acceptable, preferably non-chlorinated (chlorine suppresses yeast); let tap water stand three days in an open bucket. Dilute quince moonshine after the second distillation only with softened water containing a minimum of heavy metal salts — bottled, filter-purified, or from street machines set to maximum softening. Boiled water lacks microelements and oxygen and is the worst option for both wash and dilution.
  • Yeast (optional): from fruit collected at least a day after rain in calm weather and untreated with chemicals, quince moonshine can be made without yeast — wild strains living on the skin start fermentation. However "wild" yeast does not always activate and ferment stably, so it is better to play safe: set a berry or raisin starter 4–5 days before mincing the fruit. Cultured wine yeast (universal or for white grape juice) is optimal: it starts quickly, ferments stably and releases less foam. Spirit yeast finishes in 4–7 days versus up to 45 for wine, but aroma and taste become worse since only wine yeast releases complex esters during fermentation of 20+ days. Special fruit-wash yeast ferments less methanol thanks to pectin-destroying enzymes but otherwise equals spirit yeast. Pressed or dry baker's yeast is not recommended — it levels the moonshine aroma with its tones and is used only as a last resort.

Ingredients

  • Quince — 15 kg (weight of whole fruit)
  • Water — 15 L, plus 4 L per 1 kg of sugar
  • Sugar (optional) — up to 150 g per 1 kg of quince (optimally up to 100 g)
  • Yeast (optional) — depending on wash volume

Baker's yeast dosage: 20 g dry or 100 g pressed per 1 kg of sugar (natural in quince + added). Approximate calculations suffice: more yeast will not harm the wash or speed up fermentation.

Step-by-Step Process

1
Sort the fruit. Sort the quince: remove stems, blackened, moldy and rotten parts. For fermentation on wild yeast do not wash the fruit; in other cases at least rinse with running water. Wash ground-fallen fruit thoroughly or wipe with a dry cloth, removing soil residues.
2
Prepare the pulp. Cut each fruit in half, remove the core and seeds (they add bitterness and an undesirable almond taste). Grate the pulp with skin to a puree on a fine grater or with a meat grinder.
3
Place in vessel. Put the quince mass into the fermentation vessel.
4
Activate yeast. Activate yeast (if used) per the package instructions or according to the universal starter method.
5
Make the wash. Dissolve sugar in water. Pour the resulting syrup (or only water) into the minced quince. Stir to a homogeneous consistency. If the wort turned out thick, add more water — this will not affect the quality of quince moonshine. Pitch wine yeast or starter (optional). Fill the vessel to a maximum of 75% of volume, leaving enough free space for foam and carbon dioxide.
6
Seal and ferment. Close the wash with any airlock. Leave the vessel in a dark place at 18–28 °C. Fermentation must start within 36 hours: foam appears near the surface and the airlock begins bubbling (glove inflates). If these signs are not observed, determine why the wash is not fermenting — usually the problem is in yeast or temperature.
7
Monitor the cap. Watch the wash for the first 3–4 days so that floating pomace (pulp and skin) does not block carbon dioxide release through the airlock. Open and stir the wash periodically if necessary, submerging the pomace in the liquid. After a few days almost all pomace will settle at the bottom and stop floating.
8
Confirm completion. Depending on yeast, temperature and sugar amount, quince wash ferments 5–45 days. Signs of a finished process: almost no foam left (separate "islands" up to 3 cm in diameter may float); gas release from the airlock has stopped (glove deflated); the wort has at least partially clarified with a layer of loose sediment at the bottom; the wash smells of alcohol and is not sweet (yeast converted all sugar).

Distillation

9
Filter. Drain the fermented quince wash off the sediment through a tube, then strain through cheesecloth or a fine sieve to guaranteed remove pomace residues that could scorch and spoil the moonshine with a bitter aftertaste.
10
Assemble equipment. Set up the equipment in simple distiller mode: column height up to 60 cm with no packing inside, dephlegmator and auxiliary units off, leaving only a direct-flow condenser (coil or shell-and-tube) and one bubble trap (if provided by design) in the system.
11
First distillation. Pour the quince wash into the boiler, filling 30–70% of volume. Run the first distillation at maximum speed: the distillate may flow warm and cloudy, as long as there is no steam. Finish the process when the strength in the stream drops below 25%. Distilling "to water" is acceptable if the extra energy and time costs seem justified by a small increase in moonshine quantity.
12
Calculate. Determine the strength of the obtained raw spirit at 20 °C, or correct the value for another temperature in a moonshine calculator. Calculate the amount of absolute (pure) alcohol: volume in liters multiplied by strength in percent, then divided by 100. Dilute the distillate with water to 18–20%. To preserve organoleptics, do not clean with charcoal or any other methods.
13
Second distillation — heads. Distill the quince moonshine a second time. When the liquid boils, reduce the heating power so the distillate flows in small drops and is cold. Collect into a separate container the first 13–15% of output from the amount of pure alcohol (calculated at the previous step) — this is the "head", the initial fraction with a high concentration of harmful impurities.
14
Collect the hearts. Raise the power, achieving fast drops or a thin stream, but the drink must remain cold. Collect the "body" (drinking part) until the strength in the stream drops below 43%. Then switch off the apparatus or accumulate "tails" separately to "extract" the remaining alcohol on a rectification column in the future.
15
Dilute and rest. Dilute the quince moonshine ("body") with softened water to drinking strength — 40–45%. Pour into glass containers, close tightly and leave in a cool place for at least 3–4 days to stabilize. Shelf life is unlimited.
💡 Editor's tip: To preserve the aroma and taste of quince moonshine, reassemble the column in simple distiller mode for the second run and follow this recipe — this is exactly how real Serbian Dunevacha is made. You can add several plugs of any copper packing to the column for efficient removal of sulfur compounds; stainless packing gives no effect and only slows the distillation.

🌾 Malt Moonshine (Barley, Wheat, Rye)

Traditional malt moonshine made by hot saccharification followed by fractional distillation on a simple still. The result is a malt spirit — the alcohol base for real whisky, which can then be aged in barrels or infused on oak chips.

Category
Grain
ABV
40–45%
Difficulty
Advanced
Fermentation
3–7 days
Yield
~5 kg malt
⚠️ Legal & Safety Disclaimer: This recipe is provided for educational purposes only. Distilling alcohol at home is illegal in many countries without a licence. Always check your local laws. Read our Distillation Safety Guide before attempting any distillation.

Choosing the Right Malt

Only unfermented ("base" or "white") malt is suitable — its active enzymes can break down non-fermentable starch into simple sugars. Malt can be made at home from grain or purchased from brewing/distilling supply stores.

  • Barley malt: light grain tones of real whisky
  • Wheat malt: the softest spirit with subtle sweet aftertaste
  • Rye malt: black bread notes and slightly harsh flavor
  • Green malt (unsprouted-dried): softer than traditional dried malt, no toasted crust tones

Different malt types can be blended in one wash. A popular ratio: 50% barley, 25% wheat, 25% rye.

Ingredients

  • 5 kg malt — barley, wheat, rye, or blend
  • 20 L water — 4 L per 1 kg malt
  • Yeast: 50 g dry or 250 g pressed baker's yeast, or spirit yeast per manufacturer's instructions

Adding sugar is possible but noticeably worsens the organoleptic properties of malt moonshine — not recommended.

🛑 Safety reminder: A thermometer is mandatory for saccharification. The temperature must stay within 61–72 °C — outside this range, enzymes are destroyed or saccharification efficiency drops dramatically. Always discard the "heads" fraction during distillation. See Distillation Safety Guide.

Step-by-Step Process

1
Grind the malt. Crush to a coarse grind (if needed). Green malt with sprouts should be ground through a meat grinder or blended. If you don't have a roller mill, buy pre-ground malt — most stores offer this service.
2
Heat the water. Heat water in a pot to 50–55 °C.
3
Add the malt. Gradually add malt to the hot water, stirring until the wash is homogeneous. It is critical to eliminate all lumps and thoroughly dissolve the malt near the bottom of the pot to prevent burning.
4
Heat to saccharification temperature. Heat the wash to 65 °C, then turn off the heat and cover the pot with a lid.
5
Maintain saccharification. For the next 90 minutes, maintain the temperature between 62–70 °C, periodically reheating the pot on low heat. Stir and check the temperature every 15–20 minutes. Critical: the temperature must never go outside 61–72 °C. At higher temperatures, enzymes are irreversibly destroyed. At lower temperatures, saccharification efficiency drops dramatically. A properly saccharified wash becomes liquid (grain residue settles at the bottom) and tastes sweet (starch has been converted to sugars).
6
Optional: iodine test. Pour half a teaspoon of filtered wash onto a white saucer, add one drop of iodine with a pipette, stir with a cotton swab, and wait 30 seconds. If the solution turns purple or black, some starch remains — the darker the color, the more starch. However, if the wash tastes sweet, you can proceed; the yield will simply be lower than maximum possible.
7
Mash-out and cool. Heat the wash to 75 °C to destroy remaining enzymes that could worsen the flavor. Cover and cool as quickly as possible to below 35 °C — an ice bath or cold water bath works best. Fast cooling prevents contamination by pathogens and souring. Aim to complete cooling within 30–40 minutes.
8
Activate the yeast. Prepare yeast according to package instructions. For dry baker's yeast: sprinkle evenly over water at about 30 °C (10 ml water per 1 g yeast), cover, and wait for foam (usually 10–30 minutes). Pressed baker's yeast can be added directly from the fridge, crumbled with clean hands.
9
Transfer to fermentation vessel. Pour the cooled wash into the fermentation vessel, add yeast, and stir. Fill no more than 75% of volume. Optional: filter the wash from the grain residue (grist) and work only with the liquid part ("white" fermentation scheme). This reduces souring risk and speeds fermentation by a couple of days, but yields 5–10% less moonshine with less intense aroma and flavor. For grain washes, the "red" scheme (with grist) is preferred.
10
Ferment. Seal with an airlock and move to a dark place with stable temperature 18–26 °C. Fermentation typically takes 3–7 days. Signs of completion: no gas from the airlock, wash clarifies with sediment at bottom, smells like alcohol, tastes dry (not sweet) with slight bitterness.
11
Filter the wash. Siphon the wash off sediment through a tube, then filter through cheesecloth or a fine sieve to remove solid grain residue that could burn during heating and spoil the moonshine with bitterness.
12
First distillation. Transfer the wash to the still, filling 30–75% of volume. Distill at maximum speed — the distillate may come out warm but must be without steam. Stop collecting when the ABV in the stream drops below 25% on simple stills with minimal column height (up to 50 cm), or at 5% ABV on a column in pot-still mode.
13
Measure and dilute. Calculate the total pure alcohol: volume in litres × ABV% ÷ 100. Dilute to 18–20% ABV.
14
Second distillation with cuts. After boiling, reduce heat so the distillate drips slowly and stays cool. Collect the first 8–10% of pure alcohol volume as "heads" — discard this fraction, never consume it. Increase heat slightly and collect the "hearts" until the ABV in the stream drops below 40%.
15
Dilute and rest. Dilute the malt moonshine to 40–45% ABV. Bottle in glass, seal tightly, and rest for at least 3–5 days in a cool place to improve the flavor. Shelf life is unlimited.
💡 Editor's tip: This malt spirit is the base for real whisky. Age it in oak barrels or infuse on oak chips for 3–18 months to develop the classic whisky profile.

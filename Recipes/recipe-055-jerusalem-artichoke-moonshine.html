🌱 Jerusalem Artichoke Moonshine

A barely perceptible profile of baked apples with celery notes, and a decent yield even without added sugar — an excellent way to process a surplus harvest of earth pears.

Category
Grain / Tuber
ABV
40–45%
Difficulty
Advanced
Fermentation
3–12 days
Yield
~1 L (40%) per 10 kg
⚠️ Educational purposes only. Home distillation is illegal in many countries without a licence. Check your local laws first.

Theory: Why Inulin Matters

Jerusalem artichoke stores carbohydrates as inulin, a polysaccharide that yeast cannot ferment directly. It must first be split into simple sugars — fructose (95%) and glucose (5%). At home this is done by freezing and/or boiling with citric acid; industry uses the enzyme inulinase, which is hard to buy in small packs.

Contrary to some sources, the tubers contain at most 0.4% starch, so malt is pointless here.

Choosing the Ingredients

  • Tubers: harvest after the first frosts (October–November) or in early spring before young shoots appear; spring tubers are sweeter because part of the inulin splits naturally. Size and shape do not matter; remove rotten and mouldy parts.
  • Yield: 14–22% inulin; 1 kg of inulin converts into 1.05–1.1 kg of fermentable sugars. Theoretically 1 kg of tubers gives 176–290 ml of 40° spirit; 15–25% is lost to incomplete hydrolysis and distillation. A practical benchmark: ~1 L of 40% distillate per 10 kg of tubers.
  • Sugar (optional): 1 kg adds 1.2 L of 40% but dulls the profile with kvass/molasses tones. Up to 200 g per 1 kg of tubers is acceptable; with 10 kg+ of tubers, skip it. Dextrose or fructose in the same amount affect the organoleptics less.
  • Water: the non-boiled part must be non-chlorinated (reverse osmosis, well, spring, or tap stood 3 days). Boiled water is the worst choice for both wash and dilution.
  • Citric acid: needed for inulin hydrolysis under heat and to stabilise wort acidity; can be replaced with fresh lemon juice (5–7 g per medium lemon). Added in two stages because part is destroyed by heat.
  • Yeast: ordinary spirit yeast is best — the wort is naturally rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, zinc and vitamins; "turbo" strains are also acceptable. Baker's yeast works (7–12 days instead of 3–6, more foam) and its aftertaste is removed by proper fractional distillation. Wine yeast or berry starters are pointless: waiting up to 45 days does not improve quality.

Ingredients

  • Jerusalem artichoke tubers — 10 kg
  • Sugar (optional) — 2 kg (up to 1 kg per 5 kg of tubers)
  • Water — 6 L per 1 kg of tubers, plus 4 L per 1 kg of sugar
  • Citric acid — 8 g per 1 L of water (6 g/L at boiling + 2 g/L later)
  • Spirit or baker's yeast — per instructions; baker's: 20 g dry or 100 g pressed per 1 kg of total sugar (assume 200 g sugar per 1 kg of tubers if you cannot measure sugar content)

Wash Preparation

1
Wash the tubers free of soil, put them in a plastic bag and freeze for 48 hours so the cold partially converts inulin and ruptures cell membranes, making the tubers softer and cutting boiling time. Spring tubers may skip freezing; for autumn tubers it is strongly recommended.
2
Thaw at room temperature, then grate finely (or use a similar method) and place the mass into a cooking pot.
3
Add 4 L of water per 1 kg of tubers and citric acid at 6 g per 1 L of water. Stir to a homogeneous consistency, bring to a boil and simmer on low heat for 40–90 minutes until a homogeneous mash with a baked-apple smell and sweet taste forms. Stir and crush lumps periodically so the pulp does not scorch. Pre-frozen tubers cook about twice as fast.
4
Cool the "mash" to 30–35 °C and transfer it to the fermentation vessel. Dissolve the sugar and the remaining citric acid (2 g/L) in cold non-boiled water (2 L per 1 kg of tubers plus 4 L per 1 kg of sugar), add to the wort and stir until homogeneous and freely stirrable; add more water if needed. Non-boiled water supplies the oxygen yeast need at the start.
5
Activate spirit yeast per the label. Pressed baker's yeast can go in straight from the fridge, kneaded with clean hands; dry baker's: scatter over the surface of a sugar syrup (10 ml water and 1 g sugar per 1 g yeast) at 27–30 °C, stir after 5 minutes and leave 10–15 minutes in a dark place until stable foam forms.
6
Pitch the activated yeast (the wort must be below 30 °C). Fit any airlock and keep the vessel in a dark place at a stable 18–27 °C. For the first 3–4 days check every 10–12 hours so floating pulp and foam do not block the airlock tube; open and stir, breaking the dense surface layer, as needed. Over time the pulp settles.
7
Fermentation lasts 3–12 days depending on yeast, temperature and sugar content. It is finished when: the airlock bubbles fewer than 5 times per minute or the glove deflates; foam has disappeared (patches up to 3 cm may float); a loose sediment layer forms; the wash tastes slightly bitter without sweetness.
8
Rack the wash off the sediment (a thin tube works best), then strain through cheesecloth or a fine sieve to remove solids that could scorch during heating.

Distillation

9
Assemble the equipment as a simple pot still: column up to 65 cm with no packing, all auxiliary units off, only a direct-flow condenser (coil or shell-and-tube) and one bubble trap if the design has one. Factory columns: pot still mode.
10
Fill the boiler to 30–70% and run the first distillation at maximum speed: the raw spirit may run warm and cloudy but never steamy, otherwise losses are extreme. Stop when the stream strength falls below 20%. Running almost "to water" is acceptable if the small gain justifies the extra heating energy.
11
Measure the raw spirit strength at 20 °C (or correct for temperature in a calculator) and calculate the pure alcohol: volume (L) × strength (%) ÷ 100. Dilute to 16–20%. Charcoal cleaning is optional — understand that much of the aroma and taste will be lost.
12
Second run: after boiling, reduce power so the spirit flows in small drops and stays cold, almost like the cooling water. Collect the first 15% of the pure-alcohol amount separately as "heads" — a foul-smelling fraction with many harmful impurities, unfit for drinking.
13
Raise power smoothly so the spirit drips fast or runs in a thin stream but stays cold. Collect the "body" until the stream strength falls below 44%, then stop or collect "tails" separately to recycle them into a new wash batch or rectify them on a column.
14
Dilute the finished Jerusalem artichoke moonshine ("body") with softened water to the desired strength — usually 40–45%. Bottle in glass, seal tightly and rest for at least 5 days in a cool place. Shelf life is unlimited.
💡 To keep the characteristic baked-apple organoleptics rather than pure ethanol, run the second distillation in pot-still mode and fill the column with copper packing (SPN/RPN) to cut sulfur compounds; stainless packing gives no result and only slows the run.

🫐 Gooseberry Moonshine (Agrus, Bersen)

Gooseberry moonshine has a barely perceptible aroma and a soft berry taste with notes of green apple and white grape; sometimes grassy shades are noticeable in the aftertaste. Even experienced tasters do not immediately guess what this drink is made from.

Category
Fruit
ABV
38–45%
Difficulty
Medium
Fermentation
4–50 days
Yield
~10 kg gooseberry
⚠️ Legal & Safety Disclaimer: This recipe is provided for educational purposes only. Distilling alcohol at home is illegal in many countries without a proper licence. Always check your local laws. Read our Distillation Safety Guide before attempting any distillation.

Choosing the Ingredients

Gooseberry

Any ripe or overripe gooseberry is suitable for processing into moonshine: green (gives the richest organoleptics), red, yellow, etc. Different varieties can be mixed. Unripe berries contain much pectin — a substance from which yeast produces methanol. Poisoning with methanol from moonshine is impossible even with a gross recipe error, but it is more correct to minimize the concentration of harmful impurities during fermentation rather than relying only on fractional distillation.

Gooseberry berries sour quickly, so it is desirable to start fermentation within the first 48 hours after harvesting.

Yield: depending on variety, growing region and weather in a particular year, gooseberry contains 5–15% sugar (usually 8–11%). This is a decent sugar content: from 1 kg of berries you can realistically get 96–132 ml of 40° moonshine, but in practice up to 20% of potential alcohol is lost during double distillation.

Sugar, Water, Yeast

  • Sugar (optional): sugar increases yield (1 kg per 1.2 L of 40°) but worsens organoleptic properties with molasses and kvass tones. A compromise between quantity and quality must be found. Empirically, up to 100 g of sugar per 1 kg of gooseberry is acceptable — beyond that the moonshine becomes noticeably worse. Sugar can be replaced with the same amount of fructose or dextrose: these substances without their own taste profile only "blur" the berry taste with pure ethyl alcohol.
  • Water: gooseberry pulp releases juice poorly, and thick wash ferments slowly and is hard to filter, so water must be added. Any drinking water without chlorine is suitable: bottled, filter-purified, from wells, etc. Chlorine suppresses any yeast, so tap water is first left for 3 days in an open bucket or pot, partially evaporating the chlorine. Softened water is required for diluting the finished moonshine — with a low content of heavy metal salts: bottled or from street water machines adjusted to maximum softening. Boiled water lacks microelements and oxygen — the worst option for both wash and dilution.
  • Yeast (optional): if the berries were picked at least 36 hours after rain in calm weather, you can try making gooseberry moonshine without yeast — fermenting the wash with "wild" strains living on the skin. However, in this case the gooseberry cannot be washed, which is unacceptable for fallen berries (collected from the ground). Also "wild" yeast does not always activate quickly and ferment stably, increasing the souring risk. As a safety measure, 4–5 days before processing the harvest, set a starter on fresh berries or raisins. Cultured wine yeast suits gooseberry wash best: universal types or those intended for white grape juice. Wash on spirit yeast finishes in 4–7 days (wine ferments up to 50 days), but the moonshine loses significantly in aroma and taste, since only wine yeast releases esters during long fermentation (from 20 days) that soften berry moonshine. Special fruit-wash yeast is justified only for not fully ripe gooseberry, as it contains enzymes that destroy pectin. Pressed or dry baker's yeast is not recommended: it worsens the distillate with sharp kvass tones, added only if "wild" yeast did not start fermentation and nothing else is at hand.

Ingredients

  • Gooseberry — 10 kg
  • Sugar — up to 100 g per 1 kg of berries
  • Water — 0.5 L per 1 kg of berries, plus 4 L per 1 kg of sugar
  • Yeast (optional) — for 15 L of wort

Baker's yeast dosage: 20 g dry or 100 g pressed per 1 kg of sugar (in berries + added). Exceeding the amount will not harm but will not speed up fermentation.

Step-by-Step Process

1
Sort the berries. Sort the gooseberry: remove unripe, moldy and rotten berries. Even a small amount of spoiled raw material can cause souring or mold of the entire wash. If "wild" yeast is used, do not wash the gooseberry; in all other cases it is better to rinse with running water, especially berries collected from the ground, otherwise an unpleasant earthy aftertaste may appear. It is desirable to remove the "tails" and "tops" so the moonshine is guaranteed not to be bitter.
2
Crush gently. Crush the gooseberry by hand, crushing each berry. Do not grind to a puree state, otherwise the wash will be difficult to filter before distillation. Place the berry mass in the fermentation vessel.
3
Activate yeast. Dilute yeast (if used) per the package instructions, or activate by the universal method.
4
Make the wash. Dissolve sugar in water to a homogeneous syrup. Add the sugar syrup (or only water) to the crushed gooseberry and stir. The wort must become liquid — freely mixable by hand. If necessary, add more water; this will not affect the quality of the finished moonshine. Add wine yeast or starter (optional). Fill the vessel to a maximum of 75% of volume, leaving space for foam and carbon dioxide.
5
Seal and ferment. Close with an airlock. Transfer the wash to a dark place (can be covered) at 18–26 °C. Fermentation must start within the first 36 hours: the airlock releases gas (glove inflates) and foam appears. If this did not happen, establish the cause of why the wash is not fermenting and take urgent measures.
6
Monitor the cap. Watch the wash for the first 3–4 days: if pomace (pulp and skin residue) accumulates at the surface in a dense layer, preventing carbon dioxide release through the airlock, open the vessel and stir, submerging the pomace in the liquid. After some time almost all solid particles settle at the bottom and the wort no longer needs regular stirring — from this moment it is better not to open the wash.
7
Confirm completion. Depending on the yeast type used, sugar concentration and temperature, gooseberry wash ferments 4–50 days. Signs of a finished process: the airlock releases fewer than 5 bubbles per minute; a layer of loose sediment is visible at the bottom and the wort has noticeably lightened; almost no foam remains; the wash smells of alcohol and is not sweet to taste (yeast fermented all the sugar).

Distillation

8
Filter. Drain the fermented wash off the sediment (preferably through a thin tube like gasoline from a tank). Strain through a fine sieve or several layers of cheesecloth, removing berry residue that scorches during distillation. The wash can be clarified with bentonite to reduce impurity concentration.
9
Fill the still. Pour the wash into the distillation pot (fill 30–75% of volume).
10
Assemble equipment. Assemble the equipment in distiller mode: column height up to 60 cm with no packing inside, leave only a direct-flow cooler (coil or shell-and-tube) and optionally one bubble trap for splash protection, dephlegmator off. In factory columns the required operating mode is called pot still.
11
First distillation. Distill the gooseberry wash at maximum cooler power: moonshine may flow warm and cloudy, but necessarily without steam. End distillation at a strength in the stream below 25%. You can collect raw spirit "to water" if a small gain in quantity seems justified compared to the additional time and energy costs.
12
Calculate. Measure the strength of the obtained raw spirit at 20 °C, or make a temperature correction in an online moonshine calculator. Calculate the amount of pure (absolute) alcohol: volume in liters multiplied by strength percentage, divided by 100.
13
Dilute. Dilute the distillate with water to 18–20%, then pour into the distillation pot. Do not clean with activated charcoal or other methods, so the gooseberry organoleptics remain.
14
Second distillation — heads. Make the second fractional distillation: after the pot liquid boils, reduce the heating power so the moonshine drips slowly and is necessarily cold like the water in the system. Collect separately 14% of "heads" from the amount of absolute alcohol (calculated at step 12). This harmful fraction is not suitable for drinking.
15
Collect the hearts. Gradually increase the supplied power: moonshine must flow in a thin stream or drip fast, but remain cold. Collect the drinking part ("body") until the strength in the stream drops below 43% (distillate stopped burning in a spoon). End the distillation or accumulate "tails" separately for future processing on a column.
16
Dilute and rest. Dilute the gooseberry moonshine ("body") with softened water to drinking strength — 38–45%. Pour into glass jars or bottles, close tightly. Leave for at least 5 days in a dark cool place to stabilize. Shelf life is unlimited.
💡 Editor's tip: For the second distillation, one plug of any copper packing (SPN or RPN) can be placed in the column to more efficiently remove sulfur compounds; stainless packing gives no result and only slows distillation.

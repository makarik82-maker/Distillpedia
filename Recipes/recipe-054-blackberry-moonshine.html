🫐 Blackberry Moonshine

A light aroma of ripe berries and a slightly oily taste with notes of blackcurrant, raspberry, spices and dried fruit, finishing with pleasant tartness. The wash recipe has one critical rule: crush berries gently so the seeds stay intact.

Category
Fruit
ABV
38–45%
Difficulty
Medium
Fermentation
5–45 days
Yield
72–132 ml/kg (40%)
⚠️ Educational purposes only. Home distillation is illegal in many countries without a licence. Check your local laws first.

Choosing the Ingredients

Blackberries

Any ripe blackberries work: small, overripe, crushed, mis-shapen, long-stored or frozen — as long as there is no rot, mould or vinegar smell (a sign of souring). If the wash will run on wild or wine yeast, discard all spoiled berries: even a small amount can sour or mould the whole wash. From high-quality fruit (forest or dacha harvest) it is better to make blackberry wine.

Yield

Blackberries contain 6–11% sugar (average 8–9%); garden and hybrid varieties are sweeter than wild ones. Theoretically 1 kg of berries gives 72–132 ml of 40° spirit, but 15–20% is lost during double fractional distillation.

Sugar

1 kg of sugar adds 1.2 L of 40% distillate, but beet sugar adds kvass tones and cane sugar adds molasses notes. Empirically, up to 150 g of sugar per 1 kg of berries is acceptable (optimum 80–100 g); beyond that the berry profile is lost. Fructose or dextrose in the same amount are preferable — they have no aroma of their own and only "smear" the berry notes with pure ethanol.

Water

Blackberries give up juice reluctantly and the thick wash ferments slowly, sours easily and filters badly, so water is required. Use non-chlorinated water (chlorine suppresses yeast): reverse-osmosis, well, spring or street-dispenser water; stand tap water for 3 days in an open vessel. Dilute the finished spirit only with softened water — about 40% of the taste depends on it.

Yeast (optional)

If the berries were not treated with chemicals and picked at least 24 h after rain in calm weather, wild yeast from the skin can start fermentation — but in that case do not wash the berries. The best result comes from cultured wine yeast for red juice or universal strains; a berry or raisin starter set 4–5 days ahead is a free alternative. Spirit yeast finishes in 4–7 days but the aroma is poorer; fruit-wash yeast (with pectinase) is justified for unripe berries; baker's yeast is the worst choice — it adds a pronounced kvass profile.

Ingredients

  • Blackberries — 5 kg
  • Water — 5 L (1 L per 1 kg of berries), plus 4 L per 1 kg of sugar
  • Sugar (optional) — up to 0.75 kg (max 150 g per 1 kg of berries)
  • Yeast or starter (optional) — for 10 L of wort (baker's: 20 g dry or 100 g pressed per 1 kg of total sugar)

Wash Preparation

1
Sort the blackberries: remove rotten, mouldy and unripe berries plus the stems ("tails"). The white cores may stay — they do not affect the organoleptics, but removing them loses much juice. For wild yeast do not wash the berries; otherwise rinse with cold water.
2
Put the berries into the fermentation vessel and crush them by hand or with a wooden rolling pin. A meat grinder, blender or press is not suitable: they damage the seeds, which gives the finished spirit an intrusive bitter note.
3
Activate the yeast per the package instructions. Pressed baker's yeast may go in straight from the fridge, kneaded with clean hands.
4
Dissolve the sugar in water to a homogeneous syrup. Pour the syrup (or only water) into the berry mass and stir until homogeneous. Add the yeast or starter. Fill the vessel to max 75%, leaving space for foam and CO₂.
5
Fit any airlock and move the wash to a dark place at 18–28 °C. For the first 3–4 days check every 8–10 hours so the floating cap of pulp and skins does not block the airlock tube; open and stir as needed, submerging the cap. After a few days almost all pulp settles and stops floating.
6
Fermentation lasts 25–45 days on wine yeast or a starter, and up to 12 days on other types. It is finished when: the airlock releases fewer than 5 bubbles per minute (or the glove deflates); a loose sediment layer forms and the wort partly lightens; almost no foam remains; the wash smells of alcohol and is not sweet.
7
Rack the finished wash off the sediment (preferably through a thin tube), then strain through several layers of cheesecloth to remove solids that could scorch during distillation.

Distillation

8
Assemble the equipment as a simple pot still: column up to 60 cm with no packing, dephlegmator and auxiliary units off, leaving only a direct-flow condenser (coil or shell-and-tube) and one bubble trap if the design has one. Factory columns: pot still mode.
9
Fill the boiler to 30–75% and run the first distillation at maximum cooler capacity: the distillate may run warm and cloudy but never steamy. Stop when the stream strength falls below 25%; running "to water" is acceptable if the small gain justifies the extra energy and time.
10
Measure the raw spirit strength at 20 °C (or correct in a calculator) and calculate the pure alcohol: volume in litres × strength % ÷ 100. Dilute to 15–20%. Do not clean with activated carbon or other methods — keep the berry organoleptics.
11
Second run: after boiling, reduce power so the spirit drips slowly and stays cold. Collect the first 12–14% of the pure-alcohol amount separately as "heads" — a harmful technical fraction.
12
Raise power smoothly so the spirit drips fast or runs in a thin stream but stays cold. Collect the "body" until the stream strength falls below 43%, then stop or collect "tails" separately for future rectification.
13
Dilute the blackberry moonshine ("body") with softened water to 38–45%, bottle in glass, seal tightly and rest for at least 5 days in a cool place. Shelf life is unlimited.
💡 If mould appears but the berries have lost their shape and sorting is impossible, heat the mixed wort to 70 °C and boil for 15 minutes stirring, then cool to 28–30 °C before pitching yeast. Boiling is undesirable otherwise — it adds jam tones.

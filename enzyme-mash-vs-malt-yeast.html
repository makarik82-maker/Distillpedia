Introduction: The Terminological Confusion That Costs a Harvest

In beginner distiller communities, the question regularly comes up: "What's better for a wash — enzymes or yeast?" This question sounds logical, but it contains a fundamental error that can cost you the entire harvest of raw material, your time, and your money.

"What's better — enzymes or yeast?"
❌ The Wrong Question
Enzymes and yeast are not alternatives to each other.

They perform fundamentally different, non-interchangeable functions in the alcohol production process. Comparing them is like asking what's better for building a house:

🔨 or 🔩 A hammer or nails? One prepares, the other does. One is meaningless without the other.

This article begins with a clear delineation of roles, and then moves on to a correct and practically useful comparison: malt versus industrial enzymes for saccharifying starchy raw materials — and the place of yeast in both variants.

Chapter 1. Yeast: The Sole Ethanol Producer

🦠

Yeast Living organisms — the only source of ethanol

1.1. What Yeast Is

Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae, S. bayanus, and other species) are living single-celled organisms — fungi. They have their own metabolism: they consume sugars, breathe or ferment, reproduce, and excrete metabolic products.

The key reaction carried out by yeast:

Glucose → Ethanol + Carbon Dioxide + Heat
No enzyme, no chemical reagent, no other microorganism in home distilling practice can replace yeast in this reaction. Without living yeast cells, you will not get ethanol from sugar. Period.

1.2. What Yeast Does Besides Ethanol

As covered in detail in the article on yeast selection, during fermentation the cells produce a wide range of by-product metabolites: higher alcohols, esters, aldehydes, acids, glycerol, and sulfur compounds. It is this "trail" that forms the organoleptic profile of the distillate. Yeast is not just an ethanol factory — it is a co-author of flavor.

1.3. The Limitations of Yeast

Yeast cannot break down starch. It lacks the amylase enzymes needed to hydrolyze the α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds in the amylose and amylopectin molecules. Yeast feeds only on monosaccharides (glucose, fructose) and disaccharides (maltose, sucrose). Polysaccharides are inaccessible to it.

This limitation is the starting point for understanding the role of enzymes.

Chapter 2. Enzymes: Catalysts That Do Not Ferment

🧪

Enzymes Protein catalysts — preparers, not fermenters

2.1. What Enzymes Are

Enzymes are protein molecules — biological catalysts. They accelerate specific chemical reactions, but they themselves are not consumed in them and are not living organisms. An enzyme doesn't eat, doesn't breathe, doesn't reproduce, and doesn't produce ethanol.

Three main groups of enzymes are used in distilling:

(Termamyl, Amilosubtilin)		 Starch (internal bonds) Dextrins, short chains

(Glucavamorin, SAN Super)		 Dextrins, maltose Glucose

2.2. What Enzymes Do for the Wash

Enzymes perform the preparatory work: they convert starch, which is inaccessible to yeast, into accessible sugars. This is the saccharification stage, which precedes fermentation and is mandatory for any starchy raw material (grain, potatoes, bread).

Without enzymes or malt (which itself contains enzymes), starch will remain starch. Yeast won't touch it. There will be no fermentation.

2.3. What Enzymes Do NOT Do

✅ What Enzymes Do
  • Break starch down into dextrins and glucose.
  • Liquefy a thick mash (α-amylase).
  • Destroy pectin (pectinase), reducing methanol.
  • Prepare the substrate so yeast can work.
❌ What Enzymes Do NOT Do
  • Produce ethanol. Enzymes are not living cells and do not carry out glycolysis.
  • Reproduce. The dose added is fixed and does not increase during the process.
  • Form an aromatic profile. They do not synthesize esters, higher alcohols, or glycerol. Organoleptics are determined by the raw material and the yeast.
  • Protect against infection. They do not create an acidic environment and do not compete with bacteria.

Chapter 3. The Correct Process Scheme: Who Is Responsible for What

To dispel the confusion once and for all, here is the complete chain of alcohol production from starchy raw material:

STARCH
grain, potatoes, bread
grinding & cooking (90–100 °C) ▼
GELATINIZED STARCH
starch granules opened and accessible
α-amylase (85–90 °C) ▼
DEXTRINS
short glucose chains (liquefaction)
glucoamylase (60–63 °C) ▼
GLUCOSE
fermentable sugar (saccharification)
yeast (26–30 °C) ▼
ETHANOL + CO₂
fermentation (5–10 days)
distillation ▼
DISTILLATE
fractional heads & tails cuts
Conclusion: enzymes and yeast are at different stages of the chain and do not compete. Enzymes prepare the substrate; yeast ferments it. Both are necessary for starchy raw material.
Note on sugar wash: for a sugar wash the hydrolysis stage is not needed at all — sucrose is already a disaccharide accessible to yeast. Enzymes are useless in a sugar wash (with the exception of pectinase, if you are adding fruit).

Chapter 4. The Correct Comparison: Malt vs. Industrial Enzymes

Now that the roles are distributed, let's move on to the comparison that makes practical sense: what is better for saccharifying starch — malt or industrial enzyme preparations?

🌾 4.1. Malt: The Natural Enzyme Complex

Malt is sprouted and dried grain (usually barley) in which the plant's own enzymes are activated: α-amylase, β-amylase, proteases, and cytases.

✅ Advantages
  • Completeness. Contains amylases plus proteases (feed the yeast), cytases (break cell walls), and β-amylase (makes maltose).
  • Traditional aroma. Gives soft, bready, malty tones; melanoidins carry into the distillate (whiskey, polugar).
  • Buffering. Proteins stabilize mash pH at 5.2–5.6.
  • Availability. Sold in any brewer's shop; can be made at home.
❌ Disadvantages
  • More raw material. 150–250 g per 1 kg of starch source (15–25%).
  • Requires crushing just before mashing.
  • Temperature-sensitive. β-amylase dies above 70 °C.
  • Lower glucose yield. Leaves unfermentable dextrins (−3–5% alcohol vs. glucoamylase).
  • Time. 60–90 min with strict temperature control.

🧪 4.2. Industrial Enzymes: Precision and Efficiency

Concentrated solutions or powders of individual enzymes produced biotechnologically (usually from Bacillus licheniformis, Aspergillus niger).

✅ Advantages
  • Maximum yield. Glucoamylase fully converts dextrins to glucose (+3–5% ethanol).
  • Minimal volume. 1 ml replaces 150–200 g of malt.
  • Precise dosing for any batch size.
  • Stability. Standardized activity, batch to batch.
  • Wide temperature range. Termamyl works at 90–95 °C without scorching risk.
  • Neutral taste. Cleaner, "emptier" distillate.
❌ Disadvantages
  • No completeness. Proteins, fiber, pentosans untouched — extra yeast feeding needed.
  • No malt aroma. Impoverishes whiskey/bread profiles.
  • pH-sensitive. Glucoamylase needs pH 4.5–5.5.
  • Cost. Pricier per batch for small volumes (5–10 kg).
  • Availability. Harder to buy in some EU regions.

Chapter 5. Practical Scheme: Enzyme Mash (Step by Step)

Below is the complete technology for preparing a wash from starchy raw material using industrial enzymes. Yeast is the mandatory final stage.

1

Prepare the Raw Material

  • Grind grain, potatoes, or bread down to grits or a pulp.
  • Potatoes: wash, grind, boil 40–60 minutes.
  • Grain: crush, cook 30–60 minutes at 80–90 °C.
  • Bread: soak in hot water, heat to 80 °C.
2

Liquefaction (α-Amylase)

  • Cool the cooked raw material to 85–90 °C.
  • Add α-amylase (Termamyl, Amilosubtilin): 0.1–0.3 ml per 1 kg of raw material.
  • Stir. Hold 30–40 minutes with periodic stirring.
  • The mass will become noticeably thinner — the starch has converted to dextrins.
3

Saccharification (Glucoamylase)

  • Cool the mash to 60–63 °C.
  • Add glucoamylase (Glucavamorin, SAN Super): 0.3–0.5 ml per 1 kg of raw material.
  • Stir, wrap the vessel, and hold 60–90 minutes.
  • Check with the iodine test: yellow-brown color = starch has been hydrolyzed.
  • pH control: ensure the mash pH is 4.5–5.5. If higher, acidify with citric or lactic acid.
4

Cooling and Fermentation Prep

  • Cool the saccharified mash to 25–28 °C.
  • Add yeast nutrient (DAP, vitamins, or a specialized mix). Enzymes do not feed the yeast, unlike malt.
  • Measure the density with a saccharometer. For grain/potato mash: 10–16 °Plato.
5

Fermentation (Yeast)

  • Pitch distiller's or universal yeast: 2–3 g dry per 10 liters of mash.
  • Install the airlock.
  • Ferment at 26–30 °C for 5–10 days.
  • Yeast is mandatory. Without it, the saccharified mash will simply turn sour.
6

Distillation

  • Double distillation with fractional heads and tails cuts.
  • For potato wash: increased heads cut (12–15%) due to methanol.

Chapter 6. When Enzymes Are Better Than Malt, and Vice Versa

🧪 6.1. Choose Enzymes If:

  • Your goal is maximum yield of neutral alcohol from potatoes, bread, or grain.
  • You work with large volumes and want simpler logistics (no bags of malt to store).
  • The raw material is high-viscosity (rye, potatoes). α-amylase at 90 °C liquefies the mash more efficiently than malt.
  • You cannot precisely control mashing temperature. Termamyl forgives overheating up to 95 °C.
  • You need a clean profile without malt tones (for later rectification or infusion).

🌾 6.2. Choose Malt If:

  • You are making whiskey, polugar, bread brandy, or any drink where malt aroma is part of the bouquet.
  • You want to minimize feeding. Malt contains the amino acids and vitamins that feed the yeast.
  • You work with traditional recipes and want an authentic profile.
  • You have no access to industrial enzymes but do have a brewer's shop.

6.3. The Combined Approach

European distilleries often use a combination: malt for aroma (10–15% of raw material weight) + glucoamylase for complete hydrolysis. This gives both a malt profile and maximum yield — especially relevant for whiskey and grain brandies.

Chapter 7. Typical Mistakes and Misconceptions

Misconception Reality Solution
"Enzymes replace yeast" Enzymes break down starch but do not produce ethanol. Yeast is mandatory. Always pitch yeast after saccharification.
"Enzyme mash" is a separate wash type Enzymes are only a preparation stage. Fermentation is always done by yeast. Understand the chain: starch → enzymes → sugars → yeast → ethanol.
"The more enzyme, the better" Excess enzyme does not speed the reaction beyond its limit and can give bitterness. Follow the manufacturer's dosage.
"Enzymes work at any temperature" Each enzyme has a strict temperature optimum. Overheating inactivates it. Use a thermometer.
"Malt and enzymes are competitors" They are tools for the same task and often complement each other. Combine them for the best result.
"Enzymes produce off-odors" Enzymes produce no metabolites. Odors come from yeast and the raw material. Look for the cause in yeast or infection.
"Pectinase isn't needed for grain" Grain pentosans and β-glucans also need enzymes for full hydrolysis. Use complex preparations for rye and oats.

💰 Bottom Line

For small volumes (5 kg) the difference is negligible. For regular production (50+ kg per month), enzymes give a 30–40% saving at the saccharification stage.

Chapter 9. Legal Context

The use of industrial enzyme preparations for starch hydrolysis is permitted in the EU for the food and alcohol industries. Preparations based on Bacillus licheniformis and Aspergillus niger have GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) status and are approved by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA).

For home use, enzymes are not separately regulated. However, the very fact of distilling wash is subject to national legislation (see the previous articles in this series).

Conclusion: Not an Alternative, but a Partnership

The main conclusion of this article is simple but critically important: enzymes and yeast do not compete. They work in tandem. Enzymes convert starch into sugar. Yeast converts sugar into ethanol. Without enzymes, starch will remain starch. Without yeast, sugar will remain sugar.

The correct question for a distiller is not "enzymes or yeast?" but "malt or industrial enzymes for saccharification?" And there is no universal answer to that question — everything depends on the raw material, the goal, the volume, and the desired distillate profile.

Three Rules to Remember

🦠 Yeast Is Irreplaceable No enzyme, chemical reagent, or physical method can replace living yeast cells in producing ethanol.
🧪 Enzymes Are Preparation, Not Fermentation They break down starch but do not ferment sugars. After the enzymes' work, yeast is mandatory.
🤝 Malt and Enzymes Are Tools, Not Enemies Malt gives aroma and nutrition. Enzymes give yield and precision. Combine them consciously.

Understanding this chain turns the distiller from a person who "throws in powder and waits" into a specialist managing a biochemical process. And a specialist does not confuse a catalyst with an organism.

Happy saccharification and active fermentation.

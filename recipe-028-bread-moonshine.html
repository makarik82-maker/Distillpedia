🍞 Bread Moonshine (Without Malt, Enzymes, or Yeast)

The proposed method of preparing wash is popular in prison and army — places where it is difficult to get sugar in large quantities and any yeast. In terms of organoleptic properties, this bread moonshine resembles sugar moonshine (bread tones are almost imperceptible), but it is accessible — requires only water and bread, however you will have to wait at least a month for the wash to be ready.

Category
Grain
ABV
38–45%
Difficulty
Advanced
Fermentation
20–45 days
Yield
~5 kg bread
⚠️ Legal & Safety Disclaimer: This recipe is provided for educational purposes only. Distilling alcohol at home is illegal in many countries without a licence. Always check your local laws. Read our Distillation Safety Guide before attempting any distillation.

Theory

Yeast can only convert simple sugars into ethyl alcohol: fructose, glucose, sucrose, and maltose. Bread contains starch, whose molecules must first be broken down into simple sugars, and only then fermented. Usually starch is converted by enzymes: from malt (sprouted grain) or artificial (synthetically obtained), however mould fungi also produce alpha-amylase and glucoamylase capable of breaking down carbohydrates into sugars. This is how sake — Japanese "rice wine" — is produced.

Disadvantages of Mould-Based Wash

  • The strain of activated fungus is unknown — some, for example Aspergillus flavus and Rhizopus stolonifer, excellently convert starch into sugar, but simultaneously release toxins and other harmful substances. Although high temperature during distillation destroys potentially dangerous compounds, it is more correct to avoid their appearance in the wash.
  • Lower alcohol yield — enzymes and Koji yeast work more efficiently than mould.
  • Worse aroma and taste of moonshine — mould levels out bread organoleptics (especially wheat and corn varieties), often leaving only pronounced alcohol tones.
  • Long fermentation — up to 45 days (plus 7-12 days for mould activation) instead of the usual 5-10 days with classic saccharification or 25-30 days with Koji.
  • Higher probability of souring — due to slow fermentation, an airlock is mandatory, and there is also a risk that "wild" yeast will not activate.

Choosing the Right Bread

Any type and condition of bakery products is suitable: fresh, stale, slightly mouldy, and with other defects, but necessarily without rot, vinegar or acetone smell. Often distillers get what did not have time to be sold on time. Different varieties, for example black and white, can be combined in one wash. All else being equal, the most aromatic moonshine comes from rye bread.

Modern bakery products do not always get covered with mould, it depends on the composition. The less salt, sugar, fats, lactic and acetic acids, as well as antifungal preservatives (E280, E281, E282, E202), the higher the probability of successfully setting up the wash.

Yield

Depending on the recipe and type, bread can contain different amounts of starch (average values from total loaf weight):

  • Rye, Borodinsky, other black — 35-45%
  • Wheat — 40-55%
  • Loaves, baguettes, buns — 50-55%

Theoretically, 1 kg of starch gives 1.8 liters of 40-degree moonshine. This means that from 1 kg of bread under ideal conditions you can get 630-990 ml of distillate at 40% strength, however the practical amount will be much lower, since mould will not convert all the starch, and up to 20% of ethanol is lost during two distillations.

Ingredients

  • Bread, rusks, loaf, or bun — 5 kg
  • Sugar (optional) — up to 1 kg
  • Water — 2 L per 1 kg bread, plus 4 L per 1 kg sugar
  • Yeast (optional) — per instructions

Spirit yeast should be added according to manufacturer's instructions, and baker's yeast requires 20 g dry or 100 g pressed per 1 kg bread.

🛑 Important: The wash contains mould of unknown origin, so it is not recommended to evaluate readiness by tasting the wash. Always discard the "heads" fraction during distillation — 15% in this recipe. See Distillation Safety Guide.

Step-by-Step Process

1
Prepare the bread. Cut bakery products into 2-5 cm cubes, then together with crumbs place in a plastic bag. Evenly spray with water (50 ml per 1 kg bread or 75 ml per 1 kg rusks), seal tightly (there should be no air access), and leave for 7-12 days in a dark place at room temperature. You can proceed to the next stage when at least 80% of the bread is covered with mould. The smell will be specific but pleasant — without vinegar tones.
2
Transfer to fermentation vessel. Pour the mouldy bread into the fermentation vessel. Dissolve sugar (if used) in water.
3
Add water and mix. Pour the resulting sugar syrup or just water into the bread. Stir until homogeneous consistency. The wash should become liquid — easy to stir by hand, if necessary add more water, this will not affect the quality of moonshine in any way. Fill the vessel maximum 75% of volume, leaving enough space for foam and carbon dioxide. Optionally add baker's or spirit yeast.
4
Ferment. Close the wash with any airlock. Leave the vessel in a dark room (or cover) at 18-27 °C. For the first 3-5 days, check the wash every 12 hours so that floating bread does not clog the airlock outlet tube. If necessary, open and stir periodically. Over time, all the crumb will collect at the bottom, and by the end of fermentation almost no bread will remain — mould will process it.
5
Confirm fermentation is complete. Depending on temperature and mould activity, bread wash ferments for 20-45 days. Signs of readiness for distillation: airlock bubbles less than 5 times per minute (glove deflated); almost no foam on surface; wash at least partially clarified; loose sediment visible at bottom; alcohol smell is felt.
6
Filter the wash. Drain the fermented wash from sediment through a tube, then filter through cheesecloth or a fine sieve, guaranteed to remove solid particles that can burn during distillation, spoiling the drink with bitterness. You can also clarify the wash with white clay (bentonite).
7
Assemble the equipment. Assemble equipment in simple distiller mode: column height up to 60 cm and no packing, disable all units, leaving only one direct-flow cooler (coil or shell-and-tube) in the system, as well as one thump keg if provided by design. In factory columns, the appropriate operating mode is usually called pot still.
8
First distillation. Fill the still with wash 30-75%. Make the first distillation at maximum speed: moonshine may flow slightly cloudy and warm, just no steam. Finish distillation when strength in stream drops below 25%. Or distill "to water" if additional energy and time costs seem justified by a slight increase in quantity.
9
Measure and calculate. Determine the strength of the resulting low wines at 20 °C, or adjust the hydrometer reading for another temperature using an online moonshine calculator. Calculate the amount of pure (absolute) alcohol: volume in liters multiplied by strength percentage, then divide by 100.
10
Dilute and clean. Dilute the distillate with water to 18-20%. Optionally clean with activated charcoal.
11
Second distillation. Assemble the apparatus in pot still mode. You can place a few sections of copper RPN or SPN packing in the column to better remove sulfur impurities. Stainless steel packing is useless for these purposes and will only slow down the process.
12
Collect the heads. Distill a second time: when the liquid boils, reduce the supplied power so moonshine flows in small drops and is necessarily cold like water in the system. Pour out the first 15% of output from the amount of pure alcohol (determined at step 9). This is the "heads" — the initial fraction with high concentration of harmful impurities.
13
Collect the hearts. Collect the drinking part ("body") until strength in stream drops below 45%. Then turn off the apparatus or collect "tails" separately to process them by rectification in the future.
14
Dilute and rest. Dilute the finished bread moonshine ("body") with softened water to drinking strength — 38-45%, transfer to glass bottles or jars, seal tightly. Leave for at least 3 days in a cool place to stabilize. Shelf life is unlimited.
💡 Editor's tip: This recipe is primarily of historical and educational interest. For practical purposes, classic hot saccharification with malt, cold saccharification with enzymes, or Koji yeast are significantly more efficient. The mould-based method should only be used if no other options are available, or simply for curiosity.

Introduction: The Parameter Beginners Ignore and Professionals Worship

Imagine the situation: you bought the best grain, used quality water, pitched expensive wine yeast, and strictly maintained 28 °C. But after distillation, the spirit smells of solvent, leaves a burning aftertaste, and guarantees a heavy hangover the next morning. You check everything: the airlock is intact, the temperature is normal, the sugar has fully fermented. What is the cause?

Most likely, you overlooked pH — a parameter that remains invisible to the eye but completely dictates the rules of biochemistry inside the fermenter.

In European craft and industrial distilling practice, pH control is just as mandatory a stage as temperature measurement. The acidity level of the medium determines enzyme activity, the health of the yeast population, protection against bacterial infections, and — most importantly — the spectrum of byproducts that form. It is pH that decides whether you get a soft, drinkable spirit with a rich bouquet or a harsh low wine overloaded with fusel oils.

In this article we will examine in detail the chemistry of the process, the methods of accurate measurement, and practical ways to adjust pH for sugar, grain, and fruit washes.

Chapter 1. What pH Is and Why Yeast Loves an Acidic Environment

1.1. The Scale and the Essence of the Indicator

pH is the negative decimal logarithm of the hydrogen ion (H⁺) concentration in a solution. The scale runs from 0 to 14:

  • pH < 7 — acidic environment.
  • pH = 7 — neutral (pure water).
  • pH > 7 — alkaline environment.
⚠ Remember that the scale is logarithmic. This means a solution with pH 4.0 is 10 times more acidic than one with pH 5.0, and 100 times more acidic than one with pH 6.0. Even a small change of 0.5 units is a colossal shift in the chemistry of the medium.

1.2. The Ideal Range for Saccharomyces cerevisiae

The yeast used in distilling is evolutionarily adapted to a mildly acidic environment (its natural habitat is the surface of ripe fruit).

< 3.0 Acid shock Yeast cannot multiply; autolysis risk
3.0–4.5 Fruit zone Target for fruit and berry washes
4.5–5.5 ★ Golden zone Ideal start for sugar and grain washes
5.5–6.5 Risk zone Fusel oils rise; ester formation drops
> 6.5 Bacterial zone Infection; urethane formation risk
  • Optimal starting pH for a wash: 4.8–5.2 (for grain and sugar) and 3.5–4.5 (for fruit).
  • Optimal finishing pH (after fermentation): 3.5–4.0.

In an alkaline or neutral environment (pH 6.0–7.0), the yeast experiences stress, its cell membranes lose selective permeability, and the risk of bacterial contamination rises exponentially.

Chapter 2. The Role of pH in Yeast Metabolism and Impurity Formation

This is the most important chapter for understanding how pH affects the taste of moonshine. The acidity of the medium directly switches the metabolic pathways of the yeast cell.

🥃

Higher Alcohols (Fusel Oils) and Deamination

What happens

Higher alcohols (isoamyl, isobutyl, propyl) form during the catabolism of amino acids — the Ehrlich pathway. The yeast uses the nitrogen from amino acids to synthesize its own proteins and converts the carbon skeleton into higher alcohols.

Effect of pH

At high pH (above 5.5), the enzymes responsible for amino acid deamination work too actively. The yeast begins to process nitrogen compounds uncontrollably, producing enormous amounts of heavy fusel oils.

Result in the distillate

Sharp acetone smell, burning sensation, "heavy" hangover.

🍑

Esters and Fruity Aroma

What happens

Esters (ethyl acetate, ethyl hexanoate, isoamyl acetate) are compounds of alcohols and acids that give the distillate fruity, floral, and candy tones. The esterification reaction (combining an alcohol and an acid) is catalyzed by hydrogen ions — that is, it requires an acidic environment.

Effect of pH

In a medium with pH 4.5–5.0, ester formation proceeds at maximum efficiency. The yeast produces organic acids, which immediately bind with ethanol, forming a rich bouquet.

Result in the distillate

Soft taste, pronounced fruity and floral notes (especially important for whisky, calvados, fruit brandy).

🥚

Sulfur Compounds (Hydrogen Sulfide)

What happens

Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) smells of rotten eggs and often appears in grain and grape washes. Its formation is linked to yeast stress and the metabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids (cysteine, methionine).

Effect of pH
  • At too-high pH (> 5.5), sulfate-reducing bacteria develop and produce H₂S.
  • At too-low pH (< 3.5), the yeast experiences acid shock and autolysis (self-digestion) begins, which also releases sulfur compounds.
Balance

Maintaining the 4.5–5.0 range minimizes both risks. Moreover, in an acidic environment, heavy metals (copper, zinc) that can catalyze hydrogen sulfide formation convert into insoluble salts and precipitate out.

☣️

Safety: Ethyl Carbamate (Urethane)

What happens

Ethyl carbamate is a recognized carcinogen that can form in distillates. It arises from the reaction of ethanol with urea (released by yeast during autolysis) or with cyanides (from fruit pits).

Effect of pH

The rate of this reaction increases catastrophically in alkaline and neutral media, and at high temperatures.

Protection

Maintaining an acidic wash pH (below 4.5 at the end of fermentation) is a crucial food-safety measure that blocks ethyl carbamate synthesis.

Chapter 3. pH Dynamics: Why the Wash Must "Sour"

Many beginner distillers make the mistake of measuring pH only before pitching the yeast. In reality, pH is a dynamic indicator that should change during fermentation.

3.1. The Mechanism of the pH "Drop"

When the yeast consumes amino acids and ammonium nitrogen (from nutrient or malt), it releases hydrogen ions (H⁺) into the medium in exchange for ammonium ions (NH₄⁺). In addition, during metabolism the yeast releases organic acids: succinic, acetic, and pyruvic.

As a result, the pH of a healthy wash should drop by 1.0–1.5 units over the course of fermentation.
Example: the starting pH of a grain wash was 5.2. By the end of fermentation it should be 3.8–4.2.

3.2. What Does a Missing pH Drop Tell You?

If you set up a wash with pH 5.5, five days have passed, and the pH is still 5.4 — this is a red flag. It means one of two things:

  • Fermentation has not started or has stalled. The yeast is dead or in dormancy; there is no metabolism, so no acids are released.
  • The wash is contaminated. Bacteria (for example, lactic acid bacteria) consume acids and sugars but do not release H⁺ in the required volume, or their metabolism buffers the medium.
Rule: a consistently high pH in the middle or at the end of fermentation is a reliable sign of a problem.

Chapter 4. How to Measure pH Correctly: Tools and Methods

Measuring pH "by eye" or by taste is impossible. Accurate control requires instruments.

🧪 Litmus Paper & pH Strips

  • Pros: cheap, no calibration needed.
  • Cons: low accuracy (±0.5 unit — huge on a logarithmic scale); hard to read color in a dark grain or fruit wash.
Verdict: only for a rough check (e.g., confirming the wash has not turned alkaline). Unsuitable for precise tuning.

📟 Digital pH Meter

  • The professional standard. The device consists of a body and a glass electrode that measures the potential difference at the boundary with the solution.
Verdict: the only tool that gives the accuracy needed for quality distilling.

Rules for Working with a pH Meter

  • Calibration. The electrode "tires" over time. Calibrate the device before each series of measurements using buffer solutions (usually pH 4.01 and pH 6.86 or 7.00).
  • Temperature compensation (ATC). pH depends on temperature. Good devices have a built-in thermistor and automatically recalculate readings. If yours does not, measure pH at the temperature specified for the calibration buffers (usually 20–25 °C).
  • Electrode storage. The glass bulb of the electrode must always sit in a special storage solution (3 M KCl — potassium chloride).
  • Cleaning. After measuring in a cloudy wash, rinse the electrode with distilled water and gently blot with a tissue (do not rub!).
⚠ Never store the electrode in distilled or RO water. This leaches ions out of the glass and permanently kills the sensor.

Chapter 5. pH Correction: What and How to Use for Acidifying or Alkalizing

🔻 5.1. Lowering pH (Acidifying)

This is the most common task, especially for sugar and grain washes, and when using hard tap water with high buffering capacity.

Good
🍋 Citric Acid

Accessible and safe. Downside: the yeast can partially metabolize it during fermentation, causing an unpredictable pH rise later. Suitable for fast sugar washes.

Best for grain
🥛 Lactic Acid (80%)

The ideal choice for grain washes (whisky, bourbon). It is naturally present in malt, is not metabolized by yeast, and gives the distillate soft, creamy notes after aging.

Good
⚗️ Phosphoric Acid (85%)

Often used in brewing and distilling. Tasteless, not consumed by yeast, lowers pH excellently.

Never at home
🏭 Sulfuric Acid

Used at large industrial distilleries. Categorically not recommended at home due to the high danger of handling the concentrate and the risk of sulfate stress to the yeast.

How to add: dissolve the acid in a small amount of water and add it to the wort in small portions (1–2 ml per 10 liters), stirring thoroughly and waiting 5 minutes before re-measuring.

🔺 5.2. Raising pH (Alkalizing)

Required rarely, mainly for very acidic fruit or berry washes (e.g., blackcurrant, cranberry, or under-ripe apples), where pH can fall below 3.0, which completely blocks the yeast.

Best
🪨 Calcium Carbonate (Chalk)

Poorly soluble in water but works well in the wort. Adds calcium, which benefits the yeast and aids flocculation (settling).

Good
🧂 Potassium Carbonate / Bicarbonate

Dissolves better than chalk (potash). A reliable choice for delicate pH raises.

Not recommended
🥄 Baking Soda (Sodium Bicarbonate)

Sodium ions at high concentrations give the distillate a soapy, salty taste and suppress the yeast.

Chapter 6. pH Control Specifics for Different Wash Types

6.1. Sugar Wash

  • Starting pH: tap water often has pH 7.0–7.5 and high buffering capacity. Sugar contains no acids.
  • Action: acidify to 4.8–5.0 before pitching the yeast. Without this, the yeast starts slowly and the risk of bacterial development (which produces vomit or vinegar smells) is maximal.
  • Acid: citric or phosphoric.

6.2. Grain Wash (Whisky, Bourbon, Polugar)

Here pH control is divided into two stages: mashing and fermentation.

Stage 1: Mashing (malt enzyme activity)

  • Target mash pH: 5.2–5.4 (at room temperature).
  • Why it matters: alpha- and beta-amylase work only in this narrow range. If pH is above 5.6, tannin extraction from the husks increases (bitterness) and enzymes become less efficient. If below 5.0, the enzymes denature.
  • Correction: lactic acid, or gypsum (calcium sulfate) for hard water.

Stage 2: Fermentation

  • Target starting wort pH: 4.8–5.0.
  • Finishing pH: 3.8–4.2.
  • Why it matters: an acidic environment during fermentation is critical for preventing contamination by lactobacilli, which develop rapidly in grain wort and produce lactic acid, turning the wash into a "jelly" with the smell of rancid cheese.

6.3. Fruit and Berry Wash

  • Starting pH: fruit already contains many acids. pH can range from 2.8 (very acidic berries) to 4.0 (apples, grapes).
  • The problem: if pH is below 3.2, the yeast cannot multiply.
  • Action: measure the pH. If it is below 3.4, either dilute the wort with water (lowering acid concentration — but also sugar content) or carefully add calcium/potassium carbonate to raise the pH to 3.5–3.8.
⚠ Important: never acidify a fruit wash with mineral acids if it is already acidic. Use only chalk for delicate correction.

Chapter 7. The Effect of Water Buffering Capacity

A common mistake: distillers measure the pH of tap water (say, 7.2), add citric acid, see 5.0 on the meter, add sugar and yeast — and the next day the pH climbs back to 6.5. Why?

This phenomenon is called buffering capacity. Tap water contains dissolved bicarbonates (salts of carbonic acid). They act like a sponge: when you add acid, the bicarbonates neutralize it, and the pH barely changes until the entire "sponge" is saturated.

Solutions:
  • Use water with low buffering capacity (reverse osmosis, quality bottled water).
  • Boil tap water before use (boiling precipitates bicarbonates as scale, lowering buffering capacity).
  • Add acid not to the water, but to the already-prepared wort, doing so gradually to let the buffers "work off."

Chapter 9. Practical pH Workflow Algorithm

To integrate pH control into your practice without extra headache, follow this algorithm:

1

Prepare the water

If the water is from the tap, boil it or let it stand to reduce temporary hardness.

2

Mix the wort

Dissolve the sugar or mash the grain. Let the wort cool to 20–25 °C (the device's calibration temperature).

3

First measurement

Lower the calibrated electrode into the wort.

4

Correction

If pH is above target, add a calculated amount of acid (start with 1 ml of 80% lactic or 1 g of citric per 10 liters). Stir. Wait 3 minutes. Measure again. Repeat until you reach the target.

5

Pitch the yeast

Only after the correct pH is established.

6

Monitor during the process

Take measurements on day 2 and day 4 of fermentation. Make sure the pH is falling.

7

Final measurement

Check the pH before distillation. If it has not dropped below 4.5 and fermentation has stopped, this is a signal to look for other problems (under-fermentation, infection).

Conclusion: From Alchemy to Exact Science

pH control is the boundary that separates "artisanal" moonshining from the deliberate creation of quality spirits. Once you begin to manage the acidity of the medium, you stop guessing why this batch of moonshine turned out soft and the last one harsh. You gain reproducibility.

Three Rules to Remember

🍋 Yeast Loves an Acidic Environment A starting pH of 4.8–5.2 for grain and sugar is the foundation of population health and protection against infections.
🎼 Acidity Conducts the Flavor Low pH reduces heavy fusel oil formation and stimulates the synthesis of fruity esters. An alkaline environment will reliably give you "solvent" in the still.
📟 Invest in a pH Meter A good digital device with ATC and proper electrode storage pays for itself on the very first distillation, saving tens of kilograms of sugar and grain from spoilage.

Moonshine is not just alcohol. It is a complex chemical cocktail where every molecule has its meaning. And pH is the conductor that makes this orchestra play in harmony.

Happy measuring and clean distillations!
